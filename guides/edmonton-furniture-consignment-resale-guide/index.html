Edmonton’s Guide to High-End Furniture Consignment, Resale, and Private Sales

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Furniture consignment means a store sells your piece on your behalf. It displays the piece, keeps a commission when it sells, and pays you afterwards under the terms of its agreement. That’s different from an outright sale, where a buyer pays you in full and owns the piece from the start, and from selling privately, where you find the buyer yourself.

If you’re deciding how to sell used furniture in Edmonton, this guide explains how consignment works, what to check in an agreement, how to estimate what you’d actually receive, and when each route fits, whether you’re moving, settling an estate, or replacing a piece you’ve outgrown.

How Furniture Consignment Works in Edmonton

  1. The store decides whether to take it. Consignment stores choose pieces they expect to sell, usually by brand, condition, size, and style. Some ask for photos first.
  2. You sign an agreement. It sets the price, the commission, how long the piece stays, how and when prices drop, and what happens if it doesn’t sell.
  3. You get it to the store. Some stores expect you to deliver it; ask whether pickup is offered and what it costs.
  4. The store sells it. It displays the piece, may list it online, and lowers the price on the schedule in the agreement.
  5. You’re paid after the sale. The store keeps its commission and pays you your share on its schedule.
  6. If it doesn’t sell, the agreement decides: you collect it, it’s marked down further, or the store disposes of it.

Consignment saves you the work of finding a buyer and showing the piece. In exchange, you give up part of the price, control over markdowns, and payment until the piece sells.

What to Check in a Consignment Agreement

Every store sets its own terms, so get them in writing before you hand over the piece. Each point below changes what you receive or what you’re committing to.

  • Acceptance criteria. What the store takes and in what condition. It tells you whether consignment is open to your piece at all.
  • Starting price. Who sets it, and whether you can agree a minimum. Your share is calculated from the price it actually sells for.
  • Commission. The store’s share, usually a percentage of the final sale price. On a used sofa it’s the biggest deduction.
  • Markdown authority. How much and how often the price drops, and whether the store needs your approval. Each markdown lowers your share.
  • Delivery and pickup costs. Who moves the piece in, and who moves it out if it doesn’t sell. Paying a mover both ways can take a real share of a modest payout.
  • Contract length. How long the store holds the piece. Until it ends, you may not be free to sell the piece elsewhere.
  • Payment timing and method. When you’re paid after a sale, and how. It decides when the money actually reaches you.
  • Early withdrawal. Whether you can take the piece back before the term ends, and the fee. It matters if your plans change.
  • Unsold items. How long you have to collect, and whether the store donates, discards, or charges storage after that.
  • Damage on the floor. Who is responsible if the piece is damaged while the store has it.

Estimating What You’d Actually Receive: A Worked Example

This is a hypothetical example, not a quote from any store. It assumes:

  • A starting price of $2,000.
  • A 50% commission on the final sale price.
  • A markdown of 10% off the current price for each 30 days the piece doesn’t sell.
  • $150 to have the piece delivered to the store, and another $150 to bring it back if it doesn’t sell.
  • An early withdrawal fee of 10% of the starting price.

When it leaves versus when you’re paid

Two dates matter separately: the day the piece leaves your home, and the day you’re paid. Consignment can clear the space before the piece sells, which helps with a move or a renovation, provided a store accepts it and receives it in time. Payment still waits for the sale.

Selling Privately on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace

Selling privately on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace keeps the whole sale price and lets you decide every price change. In return, you do the work a store would: photos, a description, replies, viewings, price talks, and arranging pickup with the buyer.

  • Price it from comparable listings for the same model or similar pieces in similar condition, not from what you paid. Our guide to what a used sofa is worth covers pricing.
  • Photograph it in daylight from the front, sides, and back, with close-ups of any wear and the label.
  • Describe it plainly: brand, model, dimensions, age, and condition, including anything a buyer would find at a viewing.
  • Arrange viewings safely. Have someone else home, and keep the piece near the door if you can.
  • Get paid before it leaves. Confirm an e-transfer has actually arrived in your account, and be wary of buyers who offer to overpay or send money another way.

Which Route Fits Your Piece and Situation

No route is best for every seller. It depends on the piece and on what matters more to you: price, time, or effort.

  • Consignment suits someone who wants help selling, has a piece a store will accept, and can wait for payment until it sells.
  • A private sale suits someone willing to manage the listing, viewings, and pickup in exchange for keeping the whole price.
  • An outright sale suits someone with a qualifying piece who wants to be paid in full at pickup, for example on a deadline.

The brand and condition decide which routes are open. A sofa from IKEA, Ashley, Leon’s, or The Brick usually sells best privately, and consignment stores and resellers rarely take one. A piece from a recognized brand such as Natuzzi, Crate & Barrel, EQ3, or Rove Concepts in good condition may have all three routes available; our guide to which sofa brands hold their value covers which names buyers look for. Vintage and mid-century furniture has its own buyers, mainly specialist vintage dealers. For those pieces, and for furniture no route will take, our guide to who buys used couches in Edmonton covers the options, including donation and disposal.

If You’re Buying Pre-Owned Furniture

The same routes look different from the buyer’s side. A consignment store lets you see and sit on a piece before buying, at a price the store has set, but ask about its return policy. A private listing has the widest selection and the most negotiable prices, and inspect it carefully yourself and agree on any return arrangement before you pay. A reseller offers a smaller selection that has been inspected and described, usually with delivery. Whichever you use, our used sofa inspection guide covers what to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does furniture consignment work in Edmonton?

A consignment store sells your furniture on your behalf. If it accepts the piece, you sign an agreement, get the piece to the store, and the store displays it and lowers the price on an agreed schedule. When it sells, the store keeps its commission and pays you your share; if it doesn’t, the agreement decides whether you collect it, it’s marked down further, or the store disposes of it.

How much does furniture consignment cost?

Mainly the store’s commission, a share of the final sale price, plus any markdowns that lower that price before it sells. Some stores also charge for delivery, retrieval, storage, or withdrawing a piece early. Terms vary by store, so get them in writing and estimate your payout from the price you think it will actually sell for.

Will consignment stores pick up furniture?

It depends on the store. Some expect you to deliver the piece; others may arrange pickup, sometimes for a fee. Ask before you sign, including who brings the piece back if it doesn’t sell and what that costs.

What happens if my furniture doesn’t sell on consignment?

The agreement decides. You may have a window to collect it, the store may mark it down further, or it may donate or dispose of it, sometimes with storage charges if you don’t collect. Check these terms before you sign.

Is consignment better than selling on Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace?

Neither is better for every seller. Consignment saves you finding a buyer and showing the piece, but the store takes a commission and you’re paid only after it sells. Selling privately keeps the whole price but takes your time for photos, messages, viewings, and pickup.

How is consignment different from selling to a reseller?

A consignment store sells the piece on your behalf and pays you a share after it sells. A reseller buys it outright, usually paying when it collects the piece, and takes on the job of selling it.
