Buyer’s & Seller’s Guide

Edmonton’s Guide to Furniture Consignment, Resale, and Buying Pre-Owned

By Collin Bottrell · Edmonton Refreshed

If you’re trying to sell a sofa or sectional in Edmonton, you have more options than you might think — and they work very differently from each other. If you’re trying to buy, the same is true. Understanding how each channel operates helps you make a better decision before committing your time, your furniture, or your money.

This guide covers the three main routes: consignment stores, classified marketplaces, and curated resale. None of them is categorically better than the others — each involves real tradeoffs that are worth understanding before you pick one.

Option 1: Furniture Consignment Stores

Consignment works like this: you bring your furniture to a store, they display it, and when it sells, you receive a percentage of the sale price. You don’t get paid until the item sells. The store takes on the work of displaying, pricing, and transacting — in exchange for a cut.

Edmonton has several furniture consignment stores, ranging from broad general inventory to more design-focused curation. Terms vary by store, but the structure is fairly consistent across the category and worth understanding before you commit: items are typically consigned for a three-to-four month period; the selling price is often reduced 10% every 30 days the item remains unsold; the consignor generally receives 40–50% of the final selling price; payment is typically issued within 30 days of the sale; and if you want your item back before it sells, most stores charge a storage or early-retrieval fee.

What consignment is good for: Consignment is the right choice if your primary goal is convenience. You transport the item once, you don’t have to photograph it, manage inquiries, or coordinate strangers coming to your home, and the store handles the transaction. If you have a well-maintained piece in good condition and aren’t in a hurry, it’s a low-effort path to getting something for your furniture.

What consignment costs you: The 50% split is the most significant factor. On a sofa that sells for $800, you net $400 — and that sale might take weeks or months. The declining price structure means that if your piece doesn’t move in the first 30 days, the store will discount it, reducing your payout further. A piece that starts at $800, sits for 60 days, and sells at the 20%-reduced price of $640 returns $320 to you.

Consignment trades payout speed and percentage for convenience. For sellers who want to be done with the process and are comfortable with a lower net return, it’s a legitimate option.

Option 2: Classified Marketplaces — Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace

Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace are the highest-volume channels for used furniture in Edmonton. Any given day, there are hundreds of sofas, sectionals, and dining sets listed. For buyers, this is where the widest selection lives. For sellers, this is where the most active buyers are.

How it works for sellers: You photograph the piece, write the listing, set the price, manage all inquiries (including significant volumes of lowball offers, no-shows, and people who never respond), coordinate pickup times, and complete the transaction yourself — usually cash on pickup. There are no platform fees on Facebook Marketplace, and Kijiji’s free listings cover the vast majority of what people need.

What the marketplaces are good for: Maximum reach and maximum payout percentage. If you do the work well — good photos, accurate description, reasonable pricing — you keep everything the buyer pays. There’s no intermediary taking a cut. For sellers with the time and tolerance for the process, it’s the highest-return option.

What the marketplaces cost you: Time and friction. Managing inquiries on a well-listed item can mean dozens of messages over several days. No-shows are common. The negotiation dynamic often involves repeated lowball offers that need to be managed without antagonizing serious buyers. For larger items like sofas and sectionals, coordinating a buyer who has the right vehicle or is willing to arrange their own moving help adds another layer of complexity.

For buyers, the tradeoff runs the other direction. The selection is wide, but there’s no quality filtering or inspection. What you see in photos is not always what you get in person. Misrepresented condition, undisclosed damage, and vague descriptions are common. Recourse when something goes wrong is essentially nonexistent — all sales are final, and the platform offers no buyer protection for in-person transactions.

Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace reward effort. Sellers who invest in good photos and clear descriptions, price reasonably, and manage the process professionally will consistently get more for their furniture than any other channel. Sellers who list quickly with mediocre photos and vague descriptions will struggle.

Option 3: Curated Resale

Curated resale is a different model entirely. Instead of taking your furniture on consignment or facilitating a private sale, a curated reseller buys your piece outright — paying you immediately in cash — then takes on all subsequent responsibility: cleaning, photographing, storing, marketing, and reselling.

The tradeoff is straightforward: you get less money for the piece than you’d get selling it yourself, but you’re done the moment the transaction closes. No waiting, no managing inquiries, no coordinating strangers, no uncertainty about whether it will sell or for how much.

What sellers should know: A curated reseller’s offer will reflect the work and risk they’re absorbing. They need to clean and stage the piece, hold it in storage while it’s listed, and price it in a way that covers their costs and generates a return if it sells quickly — or a bigger return if it sits for a while. The offer they make is based on their honest assessment of what the piece will sell for and how long it will take.

This model works best for sellers who have a specific need: they’re moving and need the item gone before a deadline, they don’t want to manage the selling process themselves, or they’d rather have a guaranteed outcome than the variable return of a self-managed sale. It also works well for higher-quality pieces where the reseller can offer a meaningful price because there’s genuine resale value to work with.

What buyers should know: Curated resale offers something the other channels don’t: inspection and accountability. A reputable curated reseller has physically evaluated the piece, cleaned it, and is standing behind the description they’ve written. You’re not flying blind the way you are on Kijiji or Facebook. The selection will be narrower — a curated reseller holds a specific inventory rather than thousands of listings — but what’s in that inventory has been filtered for quality.

How to Decide Which Option Is Right for You

The right channel depends entirely on what you’re optimizing for. Here’s how the three options stack up across the factors that matter most for sellers:

Consignment Classifieds Curated Resale
Payout % ~50% of sale price 100% of sale price Immediate cash offer, lower %
Effort Low (one delivery) High (photos, inquiries, coordination) Very low (one pickup or drop-off)
Speed Weeks to months Days to weeks Immediate
Certainty Uncertain (may not sell) Uncertain (depends on effort) Certain
Quality requirement Moderate None Moderate to high

For buyers, the choice maps to different priorities. Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace offer the widest selection with no filtering and no buyer protection — you assume all risk. Consignment stores offer a walk-in retail experience with diverse inventory and staff on hand. Curated resale offers a narrower selection of inspected, quality-filtered pieces that have been evaluated and described accurately.

None of these is the objectively correct answer. They serve different priorities. Someone who needs a sofa this weekend has different needs than someone who has two months to wait for the right piece at the right price.

Regardless of which channel you use, the single variable that most affects outcome — for both buyers and sellers — is honest representation of condition. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does furniture consignment cost in Edmonton?

Consignment stores typically offer consignors 40–50% of the final selling price. The price is often reduced by 10% every 30 days the item remains unsold, so the actual payout depends on how quickly the piece sells. Most stores hold items for three to four months; pieces that don’t sell can generally be retrieved for a storage or early-retrieval fee. Terms vary by store, so confirm the specifics before signing a consignment agreement.

Is it better to sell furniture on Kijiji or through a consignment store?

Kijiji offers a higher potential payout — you keep 100% of the sale price — but requires significantly more effort: photography, listing management, fielding inquiries, and coordinating pickup. Consignment offers convenience at the cost of a 50% split and an uncertain timeline. The right choice depends on how much you value your time relative to the payout difference.

What’s the fastest way to sell furniture in Edmonton?

Selling to a curated reseller who pays cash outright is the fastest option — the transaction is complete at pickup with no waiting period. A well-priced and well-photographed Kijiji or Facebook Marketplace listing can move quickly too, typically within a few days to a week for quality pieces priced reasonably. Consignment is the slowest route, with sales often taking several weeks to months.

What types of furniture do resellers and consignment stores accept in Edmonton?

Both curated resellers and consignment stores focus on pieces in good structural and cosmetic condition, from recognizable brands, without significant odour (smoke or pets), damage, or heavy wear. The strongest candidates are pieces from brands like EQ3, Crate & Barrel, La-Z-Boy, West Elm, Natuzzi, and similar — well-made pieces that hold meaningful resale value.
