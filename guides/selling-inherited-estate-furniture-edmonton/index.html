Selling Inherited or Estate Furniture in Edmonton: What It’s Actually Worth and How to Move It

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Clearing inherited furniture goes most smoothly in this order: agree what the family wants to keep, photograph and identify the remaining pieces, note the date the property has to be empty, and then choose a route for each piece. Confirm the family’s decisions, and the executor’s if there is one, before anything is sold or collected.

This guide covers all kinds of inherited and estate furniture, from sofas and dining sets to dressers and antiques, whether you’re settling an estate or helping a parent who is downsizing.

Start With What the Family Wants to Keep

  • Walk through with the family, or share photos with those who can’t be there, and list who wants what.
  • Mark the kept pieces clearly, with tape or a list by room, so nothing goes by mistake.
  • Agree on a date by which family members collect what they’re keeping.
  • Get any decisions that affect the estate confirmed by the executor before you sell, consign, or give anything away.

Two Dates: When the Home Must Be Empty, and When You Need Payment

Keep these separate. The first is usually set by a possession date, a lease end, or a listing; establish whether it’s fixed or could shift. The second depends on the estate’s needs. Some routes clear the space before you’re paid: with consignment and auction, the furniture leaves first and the money follows the sale, though you may need to arrange transport yourself. Others pay at pickup but take longer to arrange. Knowing which date matters more for each piece narrows the choices. Our guide to selling furniture before a move covers working back from a fixed date.

How to Identify Inherited Furniture

  • Photograph the complete piece from the front, sides, and back, and any labels, stamps, or markings. Look underneath, on the back panel, inside drawers, and under seat cushions.
  • Check receipts and family records for where and when a piece was bought.
  • Record the dimensions and condition, including repairs, missing parts, and whether a set is complete.
  • Use image searches as leads, not proof. A reverse image search can suggest a maker or style, but similar-looking pieces aren’t always the same thing.
  • Get a specialist’s opinion when it could change the decision, for example a possible antique, a vintage or mid-century piece from a known designer, or a set you’re unsure about. An appraiser, auction house, or antique dealer can say whether it’s worth selling separately.

What a piece is worth depends on the maker, the design, the materials (solid wood, veneer, leather, fabric), its condition and completeness, and what comparable pieces sell for. Age alone doesn’t settle it in either direction. Our guides to judging a sofa’s quality and pricing a used sofa show how to work through comparables.

Choosing a Route for Each Piece

The right route depends on the piece and on what matters most to the family: the price, the time, or the effort.

RouteCan suitWhen you’re paid
Private salePieces buyers search for, with time to manage listingsAt pickup
Estate saleA whole household sold in the home over set daysAs items sell, or after, if a company runs it
ConsignmentPieces a store agrees to takeAfter each piece sells
Auction or specialistAntiques, collectibles, and designer or vintage piecesAfter the sale
DonationUsable pieces a charity acceptsNo payment
Giving awayUsable pieces with little resale valueNo payment
Paid removalDamaged pieces, or whatever is left at the deadlineYou pay

Private sale. Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji reach the most local buyers for everyday furniture. Each piece needs its own listing, replies, and pickup, so it suits a smaller number of pieces or a family member who can be on site.

Estate sale. Opening the home to buyers for a weekend or two can move many items at once. You can run one yourself, or hire an estate sale company, which usually prices, staffs, and advertises it for a share of the sales. Ask what happens to anything left over.

Consignment. A store sells selected pieces for you and pays a share after each sells. Ask whether the store collects or you need to arrange transport, and check the agreement for commission, markdowns, and withdrawal terms; our consignment guide lists what to ask.

Auction or specialist sale. For antiques, collectibles, or vintage and mid-century pieces from known makers, an auction house or specialist dealer can reach buyers who look for them. Ask whether they collect or you need to arrange transport, and about commission, fees, and when you’re paid.

Donation, giving away, and removal. These clear space without a sale. The next sections cover what Edmonton charities accept and what to do with pieces nobody can use.

Help with the whole clear-out. If the family would rather hand off the sorting and rehoming, a professional organizer can manage it. In the Edmonton area, Sort & Simple offers estate clear-outs and downsizing help, and rehomes belongings through donation, reuse, or recycling.

Donating Estate Furniture in Edmonton

  • Habitat for Humanity ReStore accepts clean, complete, undamaged furniture from pet-free and smoke-free homes, with free pickup in Edmonton and nearby communities. It doesn’t take sleeper sofas, mattresses, office furniture, wall units, entertainment stands, or china cabinets, and its crews can’t enter homes or move items from basements. When you book, confirm where the pieces need to be for collection.
  • Find, Homeward Trust’s furniture bank, accepts furniture in good, usable condition, including couches, sofas, and loveseats (check its list for other items), but not recliner couches, hide-a-beds, broken pieces, or furniture over 6 feet. It offers scheduled pickup in Edmonton and surrounding areas, with pickups outside Edmonton on weekends.

Both decide case by case, so send photos and book before you rely on either. Our guide to who buys used couches in Edmonton covers more donation and selling options.

Disposing of What Can’t Be Sold or Donated

Edmonton’s curbside collection doesn’t take furniture. For damaged or worn-out pieces, the City’s Eco Stations accept furniture drop-offs for a fee, and its seasonal Big Bin Events take worn-out furniture free of charge. If you can’t transport it, a junk removal company will collect it for a fee.

Confirm Each Arrangement and Set a Fallback Date

  • Confirm before you rely on it. Each of these is an arrangement: a buyer who has agreed a price, a pickup time, and how the piece will be moved; a charity that has accepted the piece and booked a date; a store that has signed an agreement.
  • Plan access. Agree who will be at the property for each pickup, and who holds the keys.
  • Set a fallback date before the home must be empty, and decide in advance what happens to anything still there: a lower price, donation, or removal.
  • Keep records of what sold, for how much, and what was donated or removed, for the estate’s accounts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if inherited furniture is worth selling?

Look at the maker, the design, the materials, its condition and completeness, and what comparable pieces sell for. Photograph any labels or markings, check family records, and compare with similar listings. If a piece might be an antique or a designer piece, a specialist’s opinion is worth getting before you decide.

Is old furniture valuable?

Age alone doesn’t decide it either way. Some older pieces are valuable because of their maker, design, or materials; many are worth more to use than to sell. Identify the piece, check its condition, and compare it with similar pieces, and get a specialist’s view if it could be an antique or from a known designer.

How do I clear a house full of inherited furniture in Edmonton?

Agree what the family keeps, photograph and identify the rest, and note the date the home must be empty. Then choose a route for each piece: private sale, an estate sale, consignment, auction, donation, giving away, or paid removal. Confirm each arrangement, and set a fallback date before the deadline for anything left.

Where can I donate estate furniture in Edmonton?

Habitat for Humanity’s ReStore accepts clean, undamaged furniture from pet-free and smoke-free homes, with free pickup, but not sleeper sofas, office furniture, wall units, entertainment stands, or china cabinets, and its crews can’t enter homes. Find, Homeward Trust’s furniture bank, accepts usable furniture including couches and loveseats, with scheduled pickup. Send photos and book with either before you rely on it.

Does the City of Edmonton pick up old furniture?

Edmonton’s curbside collection doesn’t take furniture. The City’s Eco Stations accept furniture drop-offs for a fee, and its seasonal Big Bin Events take worn-out furniture free of charge. A junk removal company can collect it for a fee.

Should we hold an estate sale?

An estate sale can suit a whole household of items sold from the home over a set period. You can run it yourself or hire an estate sale company, which usually prices, staffs, and advertises it for a share of the sales. Ask what happens to anything that doesn’t sell.
