How to Sell a Sectional Sofa in Edmonton

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

A sectional sells more easily when the listing answers a buyer’s questions before they ask: what the pieces are, how big each one is, which side the chaise is on, how it comes apart, and what collection involves. This guide covers how to work those out for an L-shaped, U-shaped, or modular sectional, whether to sell the pieces separately, and how to price it.

If you’re selling because you’re moving, list it with enough time for a buyer to measure their space and arrange a vehicle.

What to Include in a Sectional Listing

  • The full configuration: how many pieces there are, what each one is (an arm sofa, an armless seat, a corner, a chaise, an ottoman), and how they’re arranged.
  • Overall and individual dimensions: the assembled footprint and each piece’s width, depth, and height.
  • Orientation: which side the chaise or corner is on, as you face it, and any other layouts you’ve confirmed it can take.
  • The condition of every section: note wear piece by piece, and test any mechanism, such as power recline, a sleeper, or USB ports.
  • How it separates and what collection involves: how the pieces connect, which pieces are hardest to move, and any stairs or tight turns on the way out.

Photograph it assembled from the front, then from a slight angle, then each piece on its own, along with any labels and connectors. Use even lighting that shows the colour accurately. Our guide to selling furniture on Facebook Marketplace covers photos and listings in more detail.

Left-Facing or Right-Facing: Show the Orientation Clearly

Furniture makers usually name a sectional’s sides as you face it: a right-arm facing (RAF) chaise is on your right when you stand in front of the sectional and look at it. Some people describe it from a seat on the sofa, which reverses left and right, so a listing that only says “right-facing” can be read either way.

State the viewpoint in words, such as “chaise on the right as you face it,” and lead with a straight-on photo of the assembled piece. Some makers mark each piece LAF or RAF on its label, which is worth photographing. If the chaise or corner can go on either side, say so once you’ve confirmed it, as described below.

Measure the Sectional and the Way Out

Buyers check a sectional against their own room, doors, stairs, and elevator, so give them the numbers they need. Measure the route out of your home too, so you know what collection involves.

  • Assembled footprint: the length of each side, the overall depth, and the chaise length if there is one.
  • Each piece that moves on its own: its width, depth, and height. Check every section against the access route, particularly the longest, deepest, or least manoeuvrable pieces.
  • Anything removable: whether the legs unscrew or the backs come off, and the height without them.
  • Door openings: the width and height of each door on the way out.
  • Stairs: the width of the stairway, and the clearance at any turn or landing, including the ceiling height.
  • Elevator: the door width and height, and the interior width, depth, and height.

Our guide to measuring a sectional covers the sectional itself, the room, and the delivery path.

Modular Sectionals: Comes Apart or Can Be Rearranged?

Most sectionals separate into pieces, but that doesn’t mean the pieces go together another way. There are three different things to establish:

  • It comes apart for moving. The pieces separate for transport but join in only one layout.
  • It can be rearranged. The same pieces assemble in more than one layout, for example a chaise that moves to the other side, or an L-shape that becomes a straight sofa.
  • Some pieces work on their own. An armless chair or an ottoman, for example, may be usable by itself.

The two-piece Crate & Barrel Aris we bought is the first kind: its sofa and chaise separate, but the chaise fits only on the right as you face it.

The six-piece Rove Concepts Milo we bought could be set up more than one way, and two of its armless seats stood on their own.

To find out which applies to yours:

  • Identify the model from the label, the receipt, or the order confirmation.
  • Check the maker’s product page or assembly instructions for the layouts it lists and whether pieces are sold separately. If the model is discontinued, the retailer’s customer service may still have the details.
  • Look at each piece. Arms and closed sides show where another piece can’t attach: a piece with an arm on its left can’t take a module on that side.
  • Try a layout before you advertise it.

Adding matching pieces later depends on whether the maker still sells the model in the same fabric or leather, and whether new pieces will match yours after years of use. If you tell buyers they can add to it, check those first.

Selling the Pieces Separately

Splitting a sectional is a decision to make before you advertise it. Offering pieces separately can reach buyers who want a chair, an ottoman, or a smaller sofa. It can also leave you with a remainder that’s hard to sell, such as a corner piece or a chaise with nothing to attach to.

Before offering pieces separately, work out:

  • What each remaining group of pieces would be: a usable sofa, a smaller L-shape, or odd pieces.
  • Whether you’d accept the remainder selling slowly, for less, or not at all.
  • Whether you’ll look for one buyer for the whole set first, and for how long.

If you do split it, say in the listing which pieces are available on their own and which sell only together, and give each a price as well as a price for the whole set.

Pricing a Used Sectional

There’s no reliable percentage of retail for a used sectional. Price it the way you would any sofa: identify the model, compare it with the same or similar models in a similar configuration and condition, and set an asking price for how soon you need it gone. Our guide to pricing a used sofa walks through the method.

For a sectional, compare like with like: the same number of pieces, a similar size, and the same material. Asking prices show your competition, not confirmed sale values, so use sold prices where you can find them. A reduction or repost alone doesn’t tell you why the piece hasn’t sold. For a leather sectional, identify the type of leather before you compare; our guide to selling a leather sofa or sectional covers how.

Where to Sell or Rehome a Sectional

Private sale. Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji reach the most local buyers. Buyers usually collect, so say what vehicle and how many people the pieces need.

Consignment. Ask whether a store accepts sectionals and who arranges transport before you book. Our consignment guide lists what else to ask.

Donation and disposal. Charities set their own limits on size and type, which can rule out a large sectional or some of its pieces. Our guide to who buys used couches in Edmonton lists donation options, what they accept, and how to dispose of what can’t be reused.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I sell a sectional sofa in Edmonton?

List the full configuration and number of pieces, the assembled size and each piece’s size, which side the chaise or corner is on as you face it, the condition of every section, and how it comes apart. Then choose a route: a private sale, consignment, donation, or disposal for what can’t be reused.

How do I tell if my sectional is left-facing or right-facing?

Stand in front of the sectional and look at it. Manufacturers usually name the side from that view: a chaise on your right is right-arm facing (RAF). In a listing, say “as you face it” so buyers read it the same way.

What measurements do I need to sell a sectional?

The assembled footprint, including each side and the chaise, and the width, depth, and height of each piece that moves separately. Note whether the legs or backs come off. Measure the doors, stairs, and any elevator on the way out so you know what collection involves.

Can a sectional be rearranged into a different layout?

Some can and some can’t. Some sectionals come apart for moving but join in only one layout; others can be rearranged, and some pieces work on their own. Check the model’s product page or assembly instructions, look at where each piece has arms or closed sides, and try a layout before advertising it.

Can I sell just part of a sectional?

Yes, but decide before you advertise. Selling pieces separately can reach more buyers, but it can leave a remainder, such as a corner piece or a chaise, that’s hard to sell on its own. Work out what’s left after each sale, and say in the listing which pieces sell separately.

How much is a used sectional worth in Edmonton?

It depends on the model, its size and configuration, the material, and its condition. Compare it with the same or similar models in a similar configuration, using sold prices where you can find them, and set your asking price for your timeline.
