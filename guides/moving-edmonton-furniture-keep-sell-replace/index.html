Moving Guide

Moving in Edmonton? How to Decide What Furniture to Keep, Sell, or Replace

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Before a move, each piece of furniture comes down to one question: is it worth taking? That depends on whether it fits the new place, whether it still suits you, and what keeping it costs compared with replacing it. This guide covers how to decide. Once you know what you’re not taking, our guide to selling furniture before a move covers the next step.

The same questions apply whether you’re downsizing to a smaller home, moving across the city, or relocating.

Measure the New Space and the Way In

A piece has to pass two separate tests:

  • Can it get in? Measure each piece’s width, depth, and height, and check them against every door, hallway, stair turn, and elevator on the route into the new home. For a sectional that comes apart, check each section, particularly the longest and deepest.
  • Will it fit the room once it’s there? Check that the assembled piece leaves room for doors to open, for the routes people walk through the room, and for the other furniture you’re bringing.

If you can get into the new place before the move, mark each piece’s footprint on the floor with painter’s tape. If you can’t, draw it to scale on a floor plan. Our guide to measuring for a sectional covers the room and the delivery route in more detail.

Is It Still Right for You?

Wear, age, or a modest brand aren’t reasons on their own to replace something. Ask instead:

  • Is it comfortable?
  • Does it still work properly, with a sound frame, supportive cushions, and working mechanisms?
  • Does it suit the new space and how you’ll use it?
  • If it needs a repair, is the piece worth that cost to you?

A modest sofa that’s comfortable and fits can be well worth moving. A premium one that’s too big or the wrong shape for the new room may not be. Our guide to judging a sofa’s quality covers how to check a frame.

You can also keep a piece for now and replace it later, once you’ve lived in the space. That spreads the cost, which helps when the replacement budget is limited, and lets you buy for the room you actually have.

Compare the Cost of Keeping and Replacing

Put the two options side by side:

  • Keeping: the extra cost of moving the piece, plus any repairs it needs.
  • Replacing: the replacement’s price, its delivery, and any cost of disposing of the old piece, minus what you expect to sell the old piece for.

Ask your mover whether leaving a piece behind would actually lower your quote. On a fixed-price move, or one already at the mover’s minimum charge, leaving one sofa behind may save nothing. On an hourly move, ask roughly how much time the piece adds.

A hypothetical example. Your mover says leaving the sofa would take $100 off the quote, and its cushions need $200 of new foam, so keeping it costs about $300. A replacement you like costs $1,800 plus $150 for delivery, and you expect to sell the old sofa for $400, so replacing costs about $1,550. Replacing costs about $1,250 more than keeping, so it makes sense only if the new piece is worth that difference to you, for example because the replacement is more comfortable or better suited to the room. These figures are for illustration only.

Plan the Logistics Around Your Move Date

Whatever the season, the date that matters is your move.

  • Book movers early. Confirm the date, the crew size, and what the quote includes, and ask how busy they are around your date.
  • Book the elevator. If either building has one, ask management whether you need to reserve it and whether there are set moving hours or a deposit.
  • Protect furniture from the weather. Ask how the movers wrap upholstered pieces against rain or snow, and clear snow and ice from the route on both ends.

If you’re selling anything, base your listing dates on the actual move date, and decide in advance what happens to anything still unsold by a set day before it.

What to Do With the Pieces You’re Not Taking

  • Sell it privately. Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji reach the most local buyers, and you handle the listing, the inquiries, and the pickup. Our guide to selling on Facebook Marketplace covers how.
  • Sell it to a business. Some businesses buy specific kinds of furniture outright, including resellers like us that buy selected premium sofas and sectionals. Check what each one buys before you rely on it. Our guide to who buys used furniture in Edmonton covers the options, and where to donate.
  • Consign it, if a store accepts the piece. The store sells it for you and pays you after it sells, which may be after your move. Our consignment guide covers what to ask.
  • Donate it or give it away, if it’s in usable condition and a charity accepts it, or someone you know can use it.
  • Dispose of it, if nobody can use it. The City’s Eco Stations accept furniture for a fee, its seasonal Big Bin Events take it free of charge, and junk removal companies collect it for a fee.

Replacing Furniture at the New Place

Buying pre-owned can get you a piece that suits the new space for less than new. Before you sell something you still need, check what’s actually available: the dimensions, the condition, the delivery cost, and when you could have it. Otherwise you may go without a sofa between selling the old one and finding the next.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I sell my furniture before or after moving?

Sell pieces you’re sure you won’t take before the move; it frees up space and may lower what you pay to move, so ask your mover whether it would. Keep anything you’re unsure about until you’ve measured the new space, and decide in advance what happens to anything unsold by a set day before the move.

Is it cheaper to move or replace my sofa?

Compare the two. Keeping costs the extra moving charge for the sofa plus any repairs. Replacing costs the new sofa’s price, delivery, and any disposal cost, minus what you sell the old one for. Ask your mover whether leaving the sofa would actually reduce the quote; on a fixed-price move or one at a minimum charge, it may not.

Is my sofa worth moving?

If it’s comfortable, works properly, suits the new space, and is worth any repair it needs, it can be worth moving whatever its brand. A premium sofa that doesn’t fit the new room may not be. You can also keep it for now and replace it later.

How do I know if my furniture will fit in my new place?

Check two things: that each piece can get through the doors, hallways, stairs, and elevator on the way in, and that the assembled piece leaves room for doors, walking routes, and your other furniture. If you can, mark its footprint on the new floor with painter’s tape.

What can I do with furniture I’m not taking when I move?

Sell it privately, sell it to a business that buys that kind of piece, consign it if a store accepts it, donate it or give it away if it’s usable, or dispose of it through the City’s Eco Stations, a Big Bin Event, or a junk removal company.
