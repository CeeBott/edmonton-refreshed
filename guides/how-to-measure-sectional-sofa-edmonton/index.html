How to Measure a Sectional Sofa: Your Room, the Sectional, and the Way In

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Before buying a sectional, you need three sets of measurements: your room, the sectional itself, and the delivery path into your home. This guide covers how to take each one. Measurements reduce the guesswork, but they can’t guarantee a fit, so talk through any tight spots with whoever is delivering before you buy.

Measurement 1: Your Room

Overall length and depth are the sectional’s longest measurements in each direction. Together they tell you the most floor space the piece needs. For an L-shaped or U-shaped sectional, though, that isn’t the floor it actually covers. An L leaves open floor inside its corner, and that floor is part of how the room works.

Tape out the actual shape. Mark the L or U on the floor with painter’s tape, including the sofa’s depth, the chaise’s width and depth, and the open space inside the corner. For a curved or wedge-shaped corner, measure along each side of the back and tape out the angle or curve. Then walk around it and sit where you’d sit.

Clearance and walkways. Common starting points are about 18 inches between the front of the sofa and a coffee table, and 30 to 36 inches for the main routes people walk through the room. Adjust them for your household: more room for a wheelchair or walker, less for a path that’s rarely used. Check that doors, cabinets, and drawers near the sectional can still open.

Reclining sectionals. Measure the depth with every recliner fully extended, and check the manufacturer’s required clearance from the wall, which varies by mechanism. Both are usually on the product page or in the manual.

Showrooms are large, and a sectional can look smaller there than it will in your room. Take your measurements and the tape outline with you when you shop.

Measurement 2: The Sectional’s Dimensions

Listings usually give overall width, depth, and height. For a sectional, you’ll often see the length of each side of the L, for example 110 by 90 inches. Describe the orientation as you face the sectional: a chaise on your right is right-facing.

  • Overall length, depth, and height: the outer limits of the piece, including arms, legs, and back cushions.
  • Each section: the width, depth, and height of every piece that comes apart. You’ll need these for the delivery path.
  • Seat depth: from the front of the seat cushion to the back cushion.
  • Seat height: from the floor to the top of the seat cushion.
  • Arm height and back height: how high the arms and the back sit from the floor.

Comfort is personal, so compare these numbers with a sofa you already find comfortable. Measure its seat depth and seat height the same way, and if you can, sit on the piece you’re considering before you buy.

Measuring a wedge or curved corner

For a sectional with a wedge or curved corner, measure each side along the back, from the outside of the arm to the outer point of the corner. Measure the corner piece on its own too, its width across the back and its depth from back to front, and check it against the delivery path like any other section. For a curved sectional, measure the straight-line distance between the two ends and the depth at the deepest point, then tape the curve on the floor.

Measurement 3: The Delivery Path

Whether a sectional gets in depends on each section’s dimensions and shape, and on the room there is to manoeuvre it at each door, turn, and landing. Tilting or rotating a section can help, but it doesn’t establish that it will fit. Measure every point along the route, from the street or parking spot to the room.

  • Doors: the clear opening with the door fully open, measured between the narrowest points. The door itself, the stops, the handles, and the hinges can all reduce it. Measure the height as well as the width.
  • The space before and after each door and turn: how far a section can extend while it’s being angled through.
  • Hallways: the width at the narrowest point, and any corners.
  • Stairs: the width, the depth of each landing, and the ceiling height over the stairs and at any turn.
  • Elevators: the door width and height, and the cab’s width, depth, and height. Whether a section can travel lying flat or standing up depends on all of these, not the depth alone.

Common Obstacles on the Way In

Problems tend to happen where there’s no room to manoeuvre, whatever the type of home:

  • Short landings between flights of stairs, like those in many split-level and bi-level homes, leave little room to turn a long section.
  • Low ceilings over stairs, such as a basement stairway, limit how far a section can be tipped up.
  • Elevator entrances and cabs limit the size and angle of what can go in.
  • Building rules may require booking a freight elevator, padding, or set delivery hours. Ask management before you schedule.

A seller who moved the sectional through a similar home is useful to hear from, but it doesn’t prove it will fit your route. Give your measurements to the delivery crew or mover before you buy, and talk through any tight spots with them.

Quick Measurement Checklist

Your Room

  • Room length and width
  • The sectional’s overall length and depth, and its actual shape taped on the floor
  • Space to a coffee table and along walkways, adjusted for your household
  • Doors, cabinets, and drawers that need room to open
  • For a recliner: the fully reclined depth and the required wall clearance

The Sectional

  • Overall length of each side, overall depth, and height
  • Width, depth, and height of every section that comes apart
  • Dimensions with removable legs or backs taken off, where you’ve confirmed they come off
  • Seat depth, seat height, and arm height
  • Orientation, as you face the sectional

The Delivery Path

  • Clear opening width and height of every door on the route
  • Space immediately before and after each door and turn
  • Hallway width at the narrowest point
  • Stair width, landing depth, and ceiling height over the stairs
  • Elevator door width and height, and cab width, depth, and height
  • Building delivery rules and elevator booking

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if a sectional will fit through my door?

Measure the door’s clear opening: open the door fully and measure the width and height between the narrowest points, allowing for the door itself, the stops, the handles, and the hinges. Compare it with each section’s dimensions, and measure the space before and after the door, since a section often has to be angled through. Tilting or rotating a section can help, but it doesn’t guarantee a fit, so talk through tight spots with the delivery crew before you buy.

How do you measure a sectional sofa?

Measure the overall length of each side, the overall depth, and the height, then the width, depth, and height of each section that comes apart. Add the seat depth, seat height, and arm height, and note the orientation as you face the sectional, such as a chaise on the right.

What are typical sectional sofa dimensions?

They vary widely by model and configuration. On the sectionals we’ve bought, the longer side ran from 107.5 to 142 inches, heights from 29 to 37 inches, and the seat sections were 37.5 to 41 inches deep where we recorded it. Measure your room and the way in rather than relying on a typical size.

How do you measure a sectional with a wedge?

Measure each side along the back, from the outside of the arm to the outer point of the corner. Then measure the wedge piece on its own, its width across the back and its depth from back to front, and check it against your doors, stairs, and any elevator like any other section.

What measurements do I need before buying a sectional?

Three sets: your room, including the sectional’s overall length and depth and its actual shape taped on the floor; the sectional, including every section’s dimensions; and the delivery path, including each door’s clear opening, the space around turns, the stairs, and any elevator.

Will a sectional fit in my elevator?

Measure the elevator door’s width and height and the cab’s width, depth, and height, then compare them with each section. Whether a section can go in lying flat or standing up depends on all of these together, not the depth alone. Ask building management about booking the elevator, and check with the delivery crew if the fit is close.

Can a sectional be disassembled for delivery?

Most sectionals separate into sections, and on some models the legs or backs also come off. It depends on the model, so check the listing or the manufacturer’s instructions, and confirm before you buy if your route is tight.
