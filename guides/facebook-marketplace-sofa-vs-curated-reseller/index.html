Buying a Sofa on Facebook Marketplace vs. a Curated Reseller: What’s the Real Difference?

By Collin Bottrell · Edmonton Refreshed

If you’re shopping for a used sofa in Edmonton, Facebook Marketplace is almost certainly where you start. The volume is unmatched. On any given afternoon you can find dozens of listings within a few kilometres — everything from $80 IKEA Ektorps to $3,000 sectionals from sellers who are moving or upgrading. The prices look attractive. The selection is real. It makes complete sense as a first stop.

But “available” and “what you’re actually getting” are two different things. This guide breaks down what a Marketplace transaction actually involves — and where a curated reseller fills in the gaps that Marketplace structurally can’t.

What You’re Actually Getting on Facebook Marketplace

No inspection — by anyone

The seller has no incentive to disclose problems, and in most cases, no framework for identifying them either. They don’t know how to check whether the frame is solid hardwood or stapled particleboard. They can’t tell you whether the seat cushions are losing their foam density or whether the suspension webbing has stretched out. They’ve been living with the piece for years — their eyes stopped seeing it a long time ago. “Great condition” on Marketplace means “nothing is visibly falling apart from where I’m standing.” It doesn’t mean a buyer with trained eyes and good lighting would agree.

Photography hides more than it shows

Most Marketplace listings are shot on a phone, in the room where the furniture currently lives, with whatever light is available. Basements. Dim living rooms. A lamp pointed at a wall. Wide-angle phone lenses compress depth and soften surface texture in ways that are flattering to worn fabric. Stains don’t photograph well — especially on dark upholstery. Sagging seat cushions flatten out when shot from above. Armrest wear blends into shadows. A sofa that photographs as “clean and well-maintained” can look meaningfully different in person under natural light. This isn’t fraud — most sellers aren’t trying to deceive you. But the medium systematically favours the seller, and you’re making a decision based on incomplete information.

You’re getting it exactly as-is

No cleaning. No conditioning. Whatever has accumulated in those cushion crevices over the years — crumbs, pet hair, skin cells — is being transferred to your home. Most Marketplace pieces haven’t seen anything more than a surface vacuum since they left the showroom, if that. For fabric upholstery that’s been in a pet-friendly or high-traffic household, that’s a material issue. For leather, years without conditioning means a surface that’s dehydrating and more vulnerable to cracking. You’re not just buying a sofa — you’re inheriting its full history.

No recourse once the money changes hands

This is the structural reality of peer-to-peer transactions. Once you’ve e-transferred the money and loaded the piece into your truck, the transaction is over. If you get home and find a smell you didn’t notice in someone’s basement, or a structural problem that only becomes apparent when the piece is under load in your living room, there’s nothing to do. The seller isn’t a business. There’s no dispute process, no warranty, no accountability mechanism. Most sellers are honest people — but honest people can miss things, and “as-is where-is” is a complete sentence in private sales.

Logistics are entirely your problem

Renting a cargo van or truck from U-Haul in Edmonton runs $80–$120 for a day, before fuel. You need to figure out who’s helping you load and unload. You need to measure your space, measure the piece, measure your doorways and stairwells, and hope the seller measured correctly too. Edmonton homes — especially the split-levels, townhouses, and older bungalows in mature neighbourhoods — have tight entry points. A sectional that fits in a southwest suburban living room may not fit through a Glenora character home doorway. If something goes wrong in transit, that’s yours too.

The honest summary: Marketplace is a volume market. Most transactions are fine. But you are absorbing inspection risk, cleanliness risk, and logistics cost in exchange for a lower headline price. Whether that’s a good trade depends entirely on what you’re buying and how much any of those variables matter to you.

What a Curated Reseller Does Differently

The curated reseller model exists specifically to address each of those variables. Here’s how it works in practice at Edmonton Refreshed — not as a pitch, but as a factual account of what the process involves.

Frame and suspension inspection before purchase

Every piece gets physically inspected before we buy it. That means checking the frame for flex, wobble, or damage; checking seat cushion density and recovery; checking whether suspension webbing or spring systems are still doing their job. If a piece has a structural issue, we don’t buy it. The pieces in our inventory passed that filter — which means when you buy from us, the structural risk has already been evaluated by someone with an incentive to get it right.

Cleaned and conditioned as needed — or not bought at all

Part of what curation actually means is that pieces requiring heavy restoration don’t make it into inventory. If a sofa has years of embedded grime, a smell, or upholstery that needs serious work to be presentable, we pass on it. That filter happens at acquisition — before you ever see the listing. The pieces that do come through get wiped down, conditioned, and touched up as required. Some need more attention than others. The point is that someone assessed it, made a judgment call, and either addressed what needed addressing or didn’t buy it in the first place.

Photography that shows what the piece actually looks like

We shoot against a consistent backdrop — staged, lit deliberately, from angles that show the actual texture and condition of the fabric or leather. The goal is that what you see in the photos is what you get when the piece arrives. We don’t have a financial incentive to hide problems: we paid for the inventory and we want buyers to be satisfied enough to come back and refer others. Accurate photography is part of that.

Pricing against actual market value

Every piece is priced using a structured valuation process: we know what it retailed for new, we know what comparable pieces sell for in the pre-owned market, and we price accordingly. A Natuzzi leather sectional that retailed for $5,000 isn’t priced at a random number because we need to move it fast. It’s priced at what the secondary market supports. That means you can buy with confidence that you’re not overpaying — and that if you ever resell, the piece holds value.

Delivery to your door

Delivery is available for all pieces. We handle the truck, the loading, the transport, and the placement in your room. We know Edmonton’s housing stock — the tight stairwells, the low ceilings in older bungalows, the elevator dimensions in the downtown towers on 104th. If there’s a logistical complication, we deal with it. You don’t rent a truck or bribe a friend.

When Marketplace Is the Right Call

The curated model isn’t the right fit for every situation, and it’s worth being direct about that.

If you’re buying something under $500 — a basic sofa for a spare room, a sectional for a basement where your kids watch TV, a second piece you’re not planning to keep long-term — Marketplace is probably the correct choice. The margin between a $350 Marketplace find and a $700 curated equivalent doesn’t justify the premium when the stakes are low. The calculus only shifts when the piece matters: when it’s the centrepiece of your living room, when it needs to last a decade, when you care about what it looks and feels like.

Marketplace is also fine when you know exactly what you’re looking at and you’re comfortable evaluating it yourself. If you can identify a brand, assess a frame, check suspension, and you have a truck and a plan, the Marketplace savings are real. Most buyers aren’t in that position — but some are.

The cases where the curated model earns its premium: you’re spending $800 or more on a piece that’s going to be the main sofa in your home, you don’t want to deal with logistics, and you want to know that someone else has already done the vetting. For that buyer, the price difference is mostly just paying for certainty — and certainty on a large purchase in your home is worth something.

The honest framework: Use Marketplace for low-stakes, low-budget purchases where you can view in person and handle logistics. Use a curated reseller when the piece is important, the budget is meaningful, and you want the work already done for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I trust furniture photos on Facebook Marketplace?

Not without skepticism. Most Marketplace sellers photograph furniture in the room where it lives — often with poor lighting, wide-angle phone lenses that flatten perspective, and no staging. Stains don’t photograph well; neither does fabric pilling, sagging seat cushions, or worn armrest edges. A piece that looks clean in a photo can look noticeably different in person. If you’re buying from Marketplace, always view in person before committing — and bring a flashlight.

Do curated resellers charge more than Facebook Marketplace?

Typically yes — but the comparison isn’t as simple as it looks. A curated reseller’s price includes inspection, professional cleaning, accurate photography, and often delivery. A Marketplace price excludes all of that. Once you factor in truck rental ($80–$120), a helper or two, your own cleaning supplies, and the risk of a problem you only discover after the fact, the gap narrows considerably. For premium pieces — sectionals from brands like Natuzzi, La-Z-Boy, or EQ3 — a modest premium for a verified, clean, delivered piece is frequently worth it.

How is buying from a curated reseller different from buying as-is on Marketplace?

Both are as-is sales — but the difference is what’s known before you buy. At Edmonton Refreshed, every piece is inspected before purchase and photographed accurately. Any condition issue worth knowing about is disclosed in the listing, not discovered on move-in day. You’re still buying as-is; you’re just doing it with complete information. On Marketplace, you’re buying as-is from someone who may not have looked closely, in photos that may not reflect what the piece looks like in good light.
