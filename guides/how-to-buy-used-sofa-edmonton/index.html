Buyer’s Guide

How to Buy a Used Sofa or Sectional in Edmonton: What to Check Before You Buy

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Buying a used sofa or sectional goes best in this order: decide your budget, size, and how you’ll use it; ask the seller for details before you go to see it; check its condition and comfort in person; compare the total cost; and confirm access, payment, and collection before you pay. This guide covers each step, whether you’re buying on Facebook Marketplace or Kijiji, or from a store.

A viewing is usually short, often in the evening, and the seller knows the piece far better than you do. Many problems with a used sofa can be found at that viewing, with the right checks and a few more minutes.

1. Set Your Budget, Size, and Use

  • Budget: include pickup or delivery, and any cleaning or repair the piece may need, not just the asking price.
  • Size: measure the room and the route into it before you shop, so you can rule out pieces that won’t fit. Our guide to measuring for a sectional covers both, and applies to sofas too.
  • Use: think about who will sit on it and how. Seat depth and height affect whether it suits sitting upright or lounging, and pets, children, and sunlight affect which fabrics or leathers will suit you.

What Makes a Used Sofa or Sectional a Good Buy

A used piece is a good buy when the parts that are hard to replace, the frame and the way it’s built, are sound, and the wear is in places you can live with or fix. Pieces that suit buying used:

  • One you can identify. With a label or model name, you can look up what the maker publishes about its construction and what it costs new, which makes the asking price easier to judge.
  • One with replaceable wear parts, such as cushion inserts, removable covers, or legs that unscrew. Ask whether the maker still sells replacements for that model.
  • Leather with an even patina but no cracking, or fabric whose wear you can see and price in.
  • A modular sectional, whose pieces can be rearranged to suit a different room.

Take more care with:

  • Power recliners and other mechanisms. A repair may depend on parts from the maker, so test every function and ask whether anything has been fixed.
  • Smoke or pet odour, which can be in the cushion fill rather than the cover.
  • A piece with no label sold as a known brand, at a price close to that brand’s. You can’t confirm what it is.
  • Structural problems you can see, such as a seat that has dropped, a joint that moves, or a broken rail. These are repairs you’d be arranging, so price them in or walk away.

Brand is part of the answer, not all of it. A recognized maker’s published specification tells you how the model was built, and our guide to sofa and sectional brands that hold their value covers what we’ve seen from specific makers. A mass-market sofa in good condition can still be a sensible buy for a basement, a rental, or a first apartment, if the price reflects it. The checks below apply either way, because the condition of the piece in front of you is what decides.

2. Ask for Details Before You Go to See It

Photos can’t confirm a sofa’s structure or smell, but good answers can tell you whether it’s worth the trip. Ask for:

  • A photo of the maker’s label, usually under the seat cushions or underneath, and the model name if the owner knows it.
  • When and where it was bought, and the receipt if they have it.
  • The width, depth, and height, and for a sectional, each section’s size and which side the chaise is on as you face it.
  • Close-up photos of any wear, marks, or damage, and of the seat deck with a cushion removed.
  • Whether it has been in a home with smoking or pets, and whether anything has been repaired.
  • Whether it comes apart, and where it is in the house: stairs, an elevator, or a ground-floor door.
  • If it’s a long drive, a short video in daylight: someone sitting in each seat, and a slow pass over the upholstery.

With the brand and model, you can look up what the maker publishes about its construction and materials. Our brand guides, such as Natuzzi and Crate & Barrel, cover what to look for from specific makers. The model and its specifications help establish how it was built; the individual piece’s condition, fit, and price still decide whether it’s a sensible buy.

With a private seller you don’t know and can’t verify, see the piece before you send any payment or holding deposit. An established business, or a purchase you’re making from a distance, may ask for a deposit or advance payment. Before paying in those cases, confirm who you’re dealing with, since a polished listing or website doesn’t establish that on its own, and get in writing the specific piece, its disclosed condition, the total price, when payment is due, the deposit and cancellation terms, and the pickup or delivery arrangements. For a remote purchase, ask for detailed photos or a video walkthrough, though neither can tell you how it sits or smells.

3. Check Condition and Comfort in Person

See it in daylight or bright light if you can, and give yourself time to check it properly. Soft evening lamps hide stains, fading, and worn patches; a phone flashlight held low across the surface shows them.

  • Sit in every seat and every position you’ll use: upright, leaning back, and lying down if that’s how you’ll use it. Compare the support from seat to seat. A seat that sits lower or feels softer than its neighbours is worth a closer look.
  • Look at what you can reach: take the seat cushions off, check the deck for tears, and feel for loose or broken springs and sagging through the deck fabric. Check that the legs are tight and the piece sits level.
  • Watch for movement in the frame. If the seller agrees and the piece can be handled safely, with help if it’s heavy, lift one front corner a few inches. A joint that opens, a leg that shifts, or unusual flex or twist needs a closer look. It’s one observation alongside the other checks; it can’t confirm the condition of a frame you can’t see.
  • Press the arms and back, and listen. A creak, click, or movement means something is moving, such as a loose joint, a spring clip, or a mechanism. It tells you to investigate, not what the cause is, so ask the seller and look more closely before you decide.
  • Press each seat cushion down and let go. Foam should recover promptly and evenly. Cushions with a feather or down blend don’t spring back the same way and need plumping; that’s how they’re made, so judge them by whether they plump back up. If the covers unzip and the seller agrees, look at an insert: some yellowing of foam with age is normal, but foam that crumbles or sheds is worn out.
  • Test every mechanism: recliners, power controls, USB ports, headrests, and sleeper beds, through their full range.
  • Check the upholstery up close, especially the seat fronts, cushion tops, and arm tops, where wear shows first.

Our guide to judging a sofa’s quality covers the frame checks in more detail, including how to find the model’s specification.

Leather

Look for cracking, dry patches, fading, scuffs, and finish worn through to a different colour. Patina, the gradual change in colour and sheen from use, is different from damage, but cracking and worn-through finish count against condition either way. Suitable care, following the manufacturer’s instructions, may improve dryness or appearance, but don’t assume existing cracking can be corrected. On one sofa we bought, minor surface cracking remained after cleaning and conditioning. Our guide to leather sofas explains the terms sellers use.

Fabric

Look for pilling, snags, stains, and fading, and note where they are. Sun-fading doesn’t clean out, and spot-cleaning can leave its own mark. Find the cleaning code on the label before assuming a stain will come out, since the right method depends on the fabric. Our guide to fabric and bouclé sofas covers what the codes mean.

Odour

Smoke and pet odour can get into the cushion fill and foam, so cleaning the cover may not remove them. Smell it up close, at the cushion seams and the back, not from standing height. A strong scent of air freshener or fabric spray can cover an odour for a while, so if the piece smells heavily scented, ask when it was last cleaned or sprayed. If you notice an odour, weigh it as part of the piece’s condition and price; whether it can be cleaned out isn’t something you can confirm at a viewing.

Bed Bugs

Health Canada advises being very cautious with second-hand items and checking every piece you bring home. For a sofa, that means taking off the loose cushions and checking the creases, especially the seams and around the zippers; the seat and the creases along the sides and back; and the legs where they meet the upholstery and where the fabric is fastened to the frame. Look for black or brown spots, tiny white eggs, and live or dead bugs. A flashlight and the edge of a plastic card help along the seams. If you find any of these, don’t bring it home.

Sectionals

Check each piece of a sectional as if it were its own sofa: sit in every seat, check each piece for movement as you would a sofa, and look at its back and sides as well as the front. A piece that sat by a window may have faded more than the others, and backs and sides that faced a wall are easy to miss at a viewing. Make sure every connector or bracket that holds the pieces together is there, and that the pieces sit level and line up when joined. Our guide to selling a sectional explains how sectionals come apart and how their configuration is described.

4. Compare the Total Cost

Compare each option on the same three lines: the purchase price, the cost of getting it home, and any work you already know it needs. Leave out problems you’re only guessing at.

These figures are for illustration only. To judge whether a price is fair, compare it with similar pieces in similar condition, and with the current price of the same model new.

Weigh specific things, such as a seat that sits lower, fading on one arm, or a recliner that sticks, against comparable listings. 5. Confirm Access, Payment, and Collection

  • Access: check the piece against your route in: each piece’s dimensions, the clear opening of every door with the door fully open, the space at turns, the headroom over stairs, and any elevator’s door and cab. Measurements reduce the risk but can’t guarantee a fit. The measuring guide linked above covers each one.
  • Collection: decide whether you’ll pick it up yourself with a suitable vehicle and enough help, or hire movers or a delivery service. Ask the seller about stairs and parking at their end. If the seller is in St. Albert, Sherwood Park, or Spruce Grove, include the extra distance when you price pickup or delivery.
  • Weather: in an open truck or trailer, cover the upholstery against rain, snow, and road spray, and wrap leather and corners in blankets, even for a short trip across Edmonton in winter.
  • Payment: a viewing isn’t a commitment to buy. Confirm the final price after you’ve inspected the piece, then agree how and when you’ll pay, whether at collection or when it’s delivered.
  • Returns: when you buy privately, assume the sale is final and you won’t be able to return the piece, so settle any doubts before you pay.

Buying From a Private Seller or a Store

Assume a private sale is final: once you’ve paid and taken the piece home, there’s no one to return it to, so the checks above are your protection. A store or reseller may have inspected and cleaned the piece, offer delivery, or have a return policy, and the price usually reflects that work. What each one does varies, so ask what has been checked or cleaned and what the return terms are, and get them in writing. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check before buying a used sofa?

Ask for the maker’s label, the dimensions, close-ups of any wear, and its history before you go. In person, sit in every seat and position, look at the seat deck and legs, press the arms and back, test every mechanism, check the upholstery in good light, and smell it up close. Then compare the total cost and confirm how you’ll get it home.

How can I tell if a second-hand sofa is structurally sound from an online listing?

You can’t confirm it from photos alone. Ask for the label and model so you can look up its construction, photos of the seat deck and underside, its age, and any repairs. Then check it in person: sit in every seat, compare the support, and press the arms and back. Any creak, click, or movement is worth investigating before you buy.

How much should I pay for a used sofa in Edmonton?

Compare it with similar pieces in similar condition, and with the current price of the same model new. Then allow for its condition, the cost of pickup or delivery, and any cleaning or repair it needs.

Is it safe to buy a used sofa?

Buying used can be a good option if you inspect the piece and clarify the transaction before committing. Ask questions before you go. With a private seller you don’t know, see the piece before sending any payment or deposit; if a business or a remote purchase calls for a deposit, verify the seller and get the piece, its condition, the total price, payment timing, deposit and cancellation terms, and collection or delivery in writing first. In person, check its condition, smell it up close, and check the seams and creases for signs of bed bugs. Confirm the final price after you’ve seen it and agree when you’ll pay. When buying privately, assume you won’t be able to return it.

How do I check a used couch for bed bugs?

Take off the loose cushions and check the creases, especially the seams and around the zippers, then the seat, the creases along the sides and back, and the legs where they meet the upholstery and where the fabric is fastened to the frame. Look for black or brown spots, tiny white eggs, and live or dead bugs. A flashlight and the edge of a plastic card help. If you find any of these, don’t bring it home.

Should the cushions on a used sofa spring back when I press them?

Foam cushions should recover promptly and evenly. Cushions with a feather or down blend don’t spring back the same way and need plumping, which is how they’re made, so judge them by whether they plump back up. If you can see the insert, some yellowing of foam with age is normal, but foam that crumbles or sheds is worn out.

What’s the difference between cracking and patina on leather?

Patina is the gradual change in the leather’s colour and sheen from use. Cracking is a network of fine breaks in the surface, often on seat fronts, arm tops, and headrests. Cracking, dry patches, fading, and worn-through finish all count against condition. Suitable care, following the manufacturer’s instructions, may improve dryness or appearance, but don’t assume existing cracking can be corrected.

Will a used sofa fit through my door?

Measure the door’s clear opening, width and height, with the door fully open, allowing for the door itself, the stops, and the hardware. Compare it with the sofa’s dimensions, or each section’s for a sectional, and check the space at turns, the headroom over stairs, and any elevator. Measurements reduce the risk but can’t guarantee a fit.
