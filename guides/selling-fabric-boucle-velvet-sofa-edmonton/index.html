Selling a Fabric or Bouclé Sofa or Sectional in Edmonton: What the Resale Market Actually Values

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

What a used fabric sofa sells for in Edmonton depends on its brand and model, its overall condition, its size and configuration, and how its fabric has worn. Whether the label says polyester, performance, or bouclé matters less than the fabric’s condition. Check it closely, describe and photograph what you find, and price it for what a buyer will see.

What Affects a Used Fabric Sofa’s Resale Value

Buyers weigh several things together, and the fabric type is rarely the deciding one:

  • Brand and model. A named model gives buyers something to search for and to compare with its new price. A sofa with no label is compared with everything else its size and colour.
  • How the fabric has worn. Pilling, snags, stains, and fading are the first things buyers notice in photos. The sections below cover how to check each one.
  • Cushions and support. Seats that hold their shape matter as much as the fabric over them.
  • Odour. Smoke and pet odour settle into the fill and foam, and buyers ask about both.
  • Covers. Removable or washable covers, and any spare covers or extra fabric, are worth mentioning. If you’ve washed a cover, check that it still fits snugly: washing or drying it differently from the care label can shrink it.
  • Colour. A neutral colour suits more rooms. A bold one can sell well but may take longer to find its buyer.
  • Size and configuration. A large sectional suits fewer buyers, since each one needs the room and a way to collect it.

Polyester, Bouclé, and Performance Fabric: What the Label Means

Three words come up on almost every fabric sofa. They answer different questions, and one fabric can be all three.

  • Polyester (or olefin, cotton, linen, wool) is the fibre the fabric is made from.
  • Bouclé is a looped yarn that gives the fabric its nubbly texture. It can be made from polyester, wool, cotton, or a blend.
  • Performance is the maker’s claim that the fabric resists stains or cleans easily. There’s no single standard for it, so read what the maker actually says.
  • Microfiber is a very fine synthetic fibre, usually polyester. It can be woven like any other fabric, and some microfiber upholstery is finished with a soft, suede-like surface; see checking a microfiber sofa below.

Crate & Barrel’s Thrive fabric on the Aris sectional we sold is a performance fabric, 97% olefin, woven with a bouclé yarn. The Tribute fabric on the Gather we bought is a performance twill made of 100% polyester. None of these words tells you a fabric’s quality by itself. How it feels, how it has worn, and how it cleans do.

Find the cleaning code and care instructions before you clean anything. The code is on the label or the original order: W means water-based cleaners, S means solvent-based only, WS (sometimes written SW) means either, and X means vacuum only. The wrong cleaner can leave a ring or change the colour. A W code is about cleaning the fabric in place; it doesn’t mean a removable cushion cover can go in the washing machine, and a zip isn’t proof that it can. Some makers say never to machine wash or dry clean their cushion covers. Follow the instructions for your fabric and cover, including how to dry it.

How to Check a Fabric or Bouclé Sofa for Wear

Look in daylight, up close and from a few feet back. Start with the seat fronts, cushion tops, arm tops, and inside arms, where wear shows first. On a sectional, check every module.

Pilling. Small balls of the fabric’s own fibres, where it gets rubbed. A fabric shaver removes them. Test a shaver on a hidden spot first, and don’t use one on bouclé loops or velvet.

Loose fibres in bouclé. Lint, hair, and threads from clothing and blankets get caught in the loops, often in a different colour from the fabric. A lint roller or soft brush lifts most of it. It isn’t wear.

Snags and pulled loops. A loop or thread pulled up out of the weave. Don’t cut or pull it, which can unravel more. Whether it can be worked back in depends on the fabric, so follow the maker’s repair guidance or ask an upholsterer before you try. Leaving it alone, and describing and photographing it, is also fine.

Stains. Clean them by the cleaning code, or have them professionally cleaned. Spot-cleaning can leave its own mark, such as a ring or a lighter patch, so test any cleaner on a hidden spot first.

Fading. Compare the seat and arms with the back of the sofa or the underside of a cushion. Sun-fading doesn’t clean out. Our Crate & Barrel Rochelle set was clean and solid but significantly faded, and we graded it Fair.

Odour. Smoke and pet odour get into the cushion fill and foam, so cleaning the fabric may not remove them. Ask someone who doesn’t live with the sofa.

Cushions. Look for flat seat cushions, foam that stays dented, or one seat lower than the others. Feather and down cushions often recover with fluffing; the down cushions on the Fabbrica sofa we bought came back like new after eight years. Foam that has broken down doesn’t. If a seat is still low with the cushion off, the problem is in the support underneath: the springs, the webbing, or the frame. Have it looked at before you price it.

Checking a Velvet Sofa

Velvet has a short, dense pile, and the fibres lean one way. Where they’re pressed flat or brushed the other way, they catch the light differently and look lighter or darker. That shading, or crushed pile, can look like a stain when it isn’t one.

To check a mark, brush the pile gently with your hand in each direction and look from a few angles. If the mark changes or fades, it’s probably pile shading, though there can still be a stain underneath. A mark that looks the same from every angle is more likely a stain. Bare or shiny patches where the pile has worn away are permanent wear.

Crushed pile can sometimes be lifted by brushing. Before you steam or clean velvet, follow the manufacturer’s care instructions for that fabric. Some velvets shouldn’t be steamed, and the cleaning code alone doesn’t tell you whether yours can.

Before you photograph velvet, brush each cushion in the same direction so the shading reads evenly.

Checking a Microfiber Sofa

Check a woven microfiber the same way as any other fabric. On a suede-like finish, also look for flattened or matted patches on the seats and arms, shiny areas where the surface has been rubbed smooth, and rings or marks left by spills or by cleaning.

Check the cleaning code on your piece before you clean it, and use only what it allows. Test any cleaner on a hidden spot and let it dry. On a suede-like finish, brushing lightly once it’s dry can lift the surface again, and can help flattened patches too. Describe and photograph it like any other fabric sofa, with close-ups of the seats and arms.

How to Describe and Price a Used Fabric Sofa

A specific description answers buyers’ questions before they ask. Include:

  • The brand and model, and the fabric name and colour from the label or order (for example, “Tribute in Gravel”).
  • The fibre content and cleaning code.
  • The age and overall dimensions.
  • Whether the covers come off, and any spare covers or fabric.
  • Whether there are pets or smokers in the home.
  • What you found when you checked it, in plain words: “light pilling on the seat fronts, no stains or fading”.

To estimate its resale value, search Marketplace for the same model, or similar pieces from the same brand, in similar condition. A listing that sits for weeks may be overpriced, but photos, pickup arrangements, size, and limited demand can also slow a sale, so treat it cautiously as a comparable. Base the price on what a used piece in your condition is worth, not on what you paid. Our guide to what a used sofa is worth covers pricing in more detail.

Photographing a Fabric Sofa

  • Take photos in even lighting that shows the colour accurately.
  • Remove throws and pillows that aren’t part of the set, and lint-roll the fabric.
  • Show the whole piece first, from the front or a slight angle that shows its shape, then both sides and the back.
  • Get close-ups of the seat fronts and arms, and of any pilling, snags, stains, or fading.
  • For bouclé, take one close-up of the texture and one from a few feet back.
  • Photograph the label and the cleaning code.

Selling a Fabric Sofa in Edmonton: Marketplace, Consignment, or a Direct Offer

Selling on Marketplace can pay the most for the right piece, if you have time. You handle the messages, viewings, and price talks, and the buyer has to arrange the move.

Consignment hands the selling to a store, which keeps a share of the price and often marks the piece down the longer it sits.

Donation or giving it away suits a usable piece with little resale value. Charities accept furniture by type and condition, and many won’t take fabric with stains, odour, or pet hair, so check first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is bouclé furniture hard to sell?

Good condition helps, but price, size, style, and demand affect how a bouclé sofa sells, as they do any sofa. Buyers look closely at bouclé for pulled loops, matted areas on the seats, and fibres caught in the loops. Lint and loose fibres lift out with a lint roller; pulled loops and matting are wear. Photograph the texture close up and describe its condition specifically.

How do I fix a snag on a bouclé sofa?

Don’t cut or pull the loop, which can unravel more of the weave. Whether it can be worked back in depends on the fabric, so follow the maker’s repair guidance or ask an upholsterer first. Leaving it alone, and describing and photographing it when you sell, is also fine. Lint and threads caught in the loops aren’t snags; a lint roller or soft brush lifts them.

Is polyester upholstery bad for resale value?

Not on its own. Polyester is the fibre, and it’s used in everything from budget sofas to performance fabrics like Crate & Barrel’s Tribute on the Gather. Buyers care about how the fabric looks and feels now: pilling, stains, fading, and cushion wear.

What does performance fabric mean on a sofa?

It’s the maker’s claim that the fabric resists stains or cleans easily. There’s no single standard, so check what the maker says about that fabric and its cleaning code. A performance fabric can be polyester, olefin, or another fibre, and can be woven with a bouclé yarn.

How can I tell if a mark on a velvet sofa is a stain?

Brush the pile gently with your hand in each direction and look from a few angles. If the mark changes or fades, it’s probably pile shading, though there can still be a stain underneath. A mark that looks the same from every angle is more likely a stain. Before steaming or cleaning, follow the manufacturer’s care instructions for that fabric; some velvets shouldn’t be steamed.

Can pilling be removed from a sofa?

Usually, with a fabric shaver. Test it on a hidden spot first, and don’t use one on bouclé loops or velvet. Pilling can come back with use, so describe it in your listing even after you’ve removed it.

How do I clean a microfiber sofa before selling it?

Check the cleaning code on your piece and use only what it allows. Test the cleaner on a hidden spot and let it dry. On a suede-like finish, light brushing once it’s dry can lift the surface and help flattened patches on the seats and arms. Don’t machine wash removable covers unless the maker says you can.

Does fabric colour affect resale value?

It affects how many buyers a piece suits. Neutral colours fit more rooms; a bold colour can sell well but may take longer to find its buyer. Fading matters more than the colour itself: compare the seat and arms with the back or the underside of a cushion before you list.
