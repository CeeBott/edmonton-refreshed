EQ3 Sofas Edmonton: Build Quality, Canadian Manufacturing, and What to Look For Pre-Owned

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

EQ3 is a Winnipeg furniture company that designs and builds its custom upholstery at its own headquarters, which is unusual among the brands Edmonton buyers compare it with. That Canadian construction is something you can feel in the pieces.

We’ve bought and resold two EQ3 pieces, a Replay sofa and a Remi leather sofa, and this guide draws on both, alongside what EQ3 itself publishes about how it builds them. It covers where EQ3 is made, what it costs new in Canada, how it compares with other brands, where to buy it in Edmonton, and what to check on a used one.

A Brief History of EQ3

EQ3 started in 2001 as an offshoot of Palliser Furniture, the Winnipeg upholstery maker A.A. DeFehr founded in 1944, which began by selling wooden stools and tables. EQ3 applied Palliser’s manufacturing to modern designs, and the Replay, introduced in 2002, was one of its early sofas.

Who owns EQ3? The two companies have since gone separate ways. When Palliser was sold to MotoMotion in May 2026, Retail Insider reported that EQ3 was not part of the sale and continues to operate independently, though the two share history through the DeFehr family.

EQ3 sells through its own showrooms (12 in Canada as of October 2026, the nearest in Calgary), online, and through retailers such as F2 Furnishings in Edmonton.

Where Is EQ3 Furniture Made?

EQ3 says its product design, research and development, and custom upholstery manufacturing all happen under one roof at its Winnipeg headquarters, so a sofa goes from a designer’s sketch to the production line in the same building. Its sofa pages say “Custom Made in Canada”. EQ3 is open that not everything it sells is made in Canada; tables, decor, and other categories can come from elsewhere, so check the label on anything other than its upholstery.

Frame. On the Replay, EQ3 describes a kiln-dried hardwood frame, pinned, glued, and screwed in its Winnipeg factory and engineered to a 25-year structural lifespan. Our guide to judging a sofa’s frame yourself covers what to check on any used piece.

Cushions. The Replay uses layered, varied-density foam wrapped in polyester fibre, with feather-topped seat cushions and a flexible back frame.

Warranty. EQ3’s published upholstery warranty covers frames and springs on custom upholstery for 25 years, leather for five years against tearing, splitting, and cracking, fabric for one year against defects such as seam slippage (not pilling), and seat cushions for two years against collapsing. EQ3 says the coverage applies only to the original purchaser, so a used EQ3 doesn’t carry it.

EQ3 prices its sofas by size and cover grade, from more than 200 fabrics and leathers. On EQ3’s Canadian website on October 13, 2026, Grades 10 to 50 were fabrics and Grades 100 to 600 were leathers, at these prices:

The EQ3 Range: What We’ve Handled

The Replay was introduced in 2002 and relaunched in 2018 with feather-topped cushions and a flexible back frame. It’s a low, generously proportioned sofa with rounded arms and a continuous silhouette, sold as a sofa in two widths, with more configurations available in store.

The Remi was designed with New York designer Blake Tovin. It’s more structured and mid-century in feel, with a button-tufted or centre-pull back, a bench seat with fixed cushions, and stainless steel legs. Same maker as the Replay, a different design vocabulary.

EQ3’s current sofas also include the Eve, Reverie, and Cello, among others, and several collections come in modular configurations.

How EQ3 Compares to Other Brands Edmonton Buyers Consider

EQ3 vs. Rove Concepts

Both are design-led brands aimed at similar buyers. Rove Concepts is based in Vancouver, sells mainly online, and has showrooms in Vancouver and Hong Kong, so there’s nowhere to see one in Edmonton. EQ3 builds its upholstery in Winnipeg, has a showroom in Calgary, and is sold in Edmonton through F2 Furnishings. In the pieces we’ve handled from each, the EQ3 frames felt noticeably more solid.

EQ3 vs. Crate & Barrel

Both build their sofas in North America on kiln-dried hardwood frames: EQ3 in Winnipeg, and Crate & Barrel in North Carolina for collections such as the Gather and Lounge. The seats differ: the Gather we bought sat on innerspring seat cushions, where the Replay uses feather-topped foam. Crate & Barrel has an Edmonton store at Southgate; EQ3 is sold locally through F2 Furnishings.

EQ3 vs. Pottery Barn

The most obvious difference from Pottery Barn is style: Pottery Barn leans traditional and transitional, while EQ3’s range is contemporary. Pottery Barn builds many of its sofas at its Sutter Street workshop in North Carolina; EQ3 builds its custom upholstery in Winnipeg.

What Handling the Replay and Remi Teaches You

Two pieces, both bought from family homes, one six years old and one eight. Here’s what they showed us.

The weight is the first signal

The Replay is the heaviest stationary sofa we’ve handled, and it isn’t close: heavier than the Crate & Barrel Gather, the Pottery Barn Turner, and the Bracci apartment sofa, which is itself dense for its size. The crew moving it asked if it was made of granite. That weight comes from the hardwood frame and the scale of the piece.

The Remi is shorter and lighter, but it has the same solid feel when moved. Neither frame flexed or made noise under stress.

The Replay reads better in person

The Replay’s continuous silhouette, with straight lines, rounded corners, and nearly seamless upholstery, photographs well and looks even better in a room. The tall Black Ash legs lift it visually, so a 99-inch sofa looks considered rather than simply large.

The Remi is a different register: structured, with a button-tufted leather back, stainless steel legs, and arms that wrap over the seat. Where the Replay is about continuous form, the Remi is about detail. They suit different rooms.

The fabric story is more complicated than the label suggests

The Replay we handled was upholstered in a 100% polyester fabric with the texture and feel of thick wool. It cleaned well and felt high quality. Pilling, though, was present across the contact surfaces, the arms and seat edges especially, on a six-year-old piece used by a family. EQ3’s fabric warranty doesn’t cover pilling, so on any used EQ3 fabric piece, the arms and seat edges are the first places to look.

Feather-topped cushions: the comfort and the maintenance

The Replay’s seat cushions have a foam core topped with a feather layer. The result is a dense, substantial seat that suits people who like a firmer sit. It isn’t the plush sink of a deep sofa with loose down cushions; the Replay sits you more upright and holds you more firmly.

The feather topping needs regular fluffing, and EQ3’s care guidance says so: it recommends massaging seats and backs to keep the fill from matting. Without it, the fill compresses unevenly at the spots people sit, which we saw signs of on the piece we handled, otherwise in very good shape. If a cushion stays flat or uneven after shaking and working it, look more closely and check how it supports you when seated; fluffing alone won’t tell you whether the problem is compressed filling or worn foam. The back cushions use a feather and fibre blend, softer than the seats, on a flexible back frame.

The Remi in leather: better in person than in photos

The Remi we handled was eight years old, in grey top-grain leather with a button-tufted back and fixed seat and back cushions. The leather had stretched at the flex points of the tufted back, which EQ3 describes as normal for leather and for bench seats over time, and that stretching looks more dramatic in photos than in person. In the room, the piece looked well used and well kept, not damaged.

Its leather has a smoother, glossier finish than the waxed leather on the Pottery Barn Turner, and it reads as more polished and less natural. A finished leather like this ages more evenly and shows less of the hide’s markings than an aniline or waxed one. Neither is better; they’re different choices.

With fixed cushions, you can’t remove them to check the fill, so you judge the leather, how firm the seat feels under your weight, and how the frame feels. On the piece we handled, the foam showed reasonable compression for eight years of family use: not soft, not collapsed.

Check the stainless steel legs

One of the Remi’s stainless steel legs looked slightly bent from an old impact, visible if you looked for it but not enough to affect how it sat. The sofa was level and stable. From one piece we can’t say whether the slim legs are vulnerable or this one was simply knocked. On any used Remi or similar EQ3 piece, check the legs and confirm it sits flat on all four.

Where to Buy EQ3 in Edmonton

There’s no EQ3 showroom in Edmonton. New pieces are sold locally through F2 Furnishings, whose brands page lists EQ3; our guide to Edmonton’s high-end furniture stores covers what else it carries. EQ3 also sells online at EQ3.com, and its nearest showroom is in Calgary, at Deerfoot Meadows.

Buying new gets you the exact cover and configuration you want, plus EQ3’s warranty. Buying used means a lower price, a piece you can see in its real condition, and no warranty, since EQ3’s coverage stays with the original purchaser. On models EQ3 has made for years, like the Replay and Remi, a used piece can have plenty of life left; the checks below help you tell.

What to Look for in a Used EQ3

Buying any used sofa in Edmonton comes down to a handful of checks. Here’s what’s specific to EQ3.

  • Fabric pilling, especially on the arms. Run your hand across the arms and seat edges in good light before judging the fabric. On the Replay we handled, pilling was throughout and heaviest on the arms. That may reflect how that owner used it more than the brand, but it was significant enough to weigh when judging whether a used Replay’s price is fair.
  • The EQ3 tag. On both pieces we handled, a red or black plastic tag stuck out from the lower back-corner seam on the left side. It’s a useful way to confirm what you’re looking at.
  • Whether the cushions recover. On feather-topped or feather-filled cushions like the Replay’s, shaking and working the cushion should restore its shape. If the cushion stays flat or uneven after fluffing, inspect it more closely and check how it supports you when seated. Fluffing alone won’t tell you whether the problem is compressed filling or worn foam. EQ3’s cushion warranty runs two years and only for the original buyer, so on a used piece, your own inspection is the evidence you have.
  • The legs. The Replay’s Black Ash legs should be intact, tight, and free of cracks. Stainless steel legs on the Remi and similar models should be straight; confirm the piece sits level.
  • Age and household. Ask about pets, children, smoke, and sun, and look at the fabric and cushions with those answers in mind. A well-kept EQ3 fabric piece at seven or eight years can still be in good condition; one in heavy daily use by a sunny window may be at the end of its upholstery life even if the frame has decades left. Our guide to furniture condition grades explains how we grade wear.
  • The label. EQ3 makes its custom upholstery in Winnipeg, so the label on an EQ3 sofa should show Canadian manufacture. If a piece sold as EQ3 says otherwise, ask why.

Is EQ3 Good Quality?

On the two we’ve handled, yes, where it counts most. Both frames were solid at six and eight years old, and EQ3 builds its custom upholstery in its own Winnipeg factory with a 25-year frame warranty behind it. The covers are where pieces vary: the Replay’s polyester fabric pilled with family use, and the Remi’s leather had stretched and worn in. Both frames we handled remained solid, but check the frame, cushions, and upholstery on the individual piece you’re considering.
