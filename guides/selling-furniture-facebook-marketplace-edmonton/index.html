Selling Furniture on Facebook Marketplace in Edmonton: What They Don’t Tell You

By Collin Bottrell · Edmonton Refreshed

Facebook Marketplace has a genuinely good pitch. It’s free to list, it’s fast to set up, and a large portion of Edmonton is already on it. When you have a sofa you need to sell, it feels like the obvious first move.

What it doesn’t tell you is what the next two to four weeks actually look like.

This isn’t an argument against Marketplace — it’s a realistic picture of what selling a quality piece of furniture on it actually involves, so you can decide whether that tradeoff makes sense for you.

The Listing Is the Easy Part

Writing the listing takes effort: you need good photos, a description that’s accurate without burying the lead, and a price.

And then you wait.

The first wave of messages usually arrives within a few hours of a new listing. On a $3,000 sofa, the breakdown looks roughly like this: a few genuine inquiries, a larger number of people asking “is this still available?” who will never respond again, and a steady stream of offers ranging from optimistic lowballs to genuinely insulting ones. For a $3,000 couch, offers of $800 are not uncommon. Neither are $1,200. Neither is the message that says “will you take $1,500 firm?” from someone you never hear from again after you say yes.

This is not unusual. It is the norm.

The Time Cost Is Real and It Compounds

A typical furniture sale on Marketplace takes 2–4 weeks from listing to completed handoff, assuming it sells at all. Here’s what that time looks like in practice.

Week 1: High activity. You’re responding to messages daily, sometimes multiple times per day. Most don’t go anywhere. You field 3–5 serious inquiries. Two schedule viewings, one doesn’t show.

Week 2: Activity drops. The algorithm is burying your listing because engagement has slowed. You relist or refresh. New wave of messages, similar conversion rate. One person schedules a pickup for Saturday, asks you to hold it until then. You decline two other inquiries in the meantime. They cancel Thursday evening.

Week 3: You’re now considering whether your price is the problem. Maybe you drop $200. Activity picks up slightly. Someone commits, asks if Sunday works. You agree. They message Saturday to ask if you can do Monday instead. You’re starting to dislike this process.

Week 4: It sells, or it doesn’t. If it does, you coordinate a pickup window, you’re home during it, and you spend 15 minutes helping a stranger load a 200-pound sectional into a vehicle that may or may not be adequately sized for the job.

None of this is catastrophic. But none of it is free either. The hours add up. And if you’re selling a piece you care about — something you paid real money for, something that was part of your home — the emotional weight of the process adds something that’s harder to quantify.

The Pricing Pressure Is Structural

Marketplace has a pricing dynamic that works against sellers of quality furniture. Buyers come to the platform expecting deals, and the algorithm rewards listings that move quickly. A listing that sits generates less engagement, which reduces visibility, which reduces inquiries, which extends the timeline — which often leads sellers to drop the price. The platform structurally incentivizes underpricing.

For a $500 sofa, this is a manageable friction. For a $3,000–5,000 piece, it becomes a meaningful financial loss. You may end up taking $1,400 for something that should have sold for $2,200 — not because the piece wasn’t worth it, but because the process ground you down.

The buyers who close on Marketplace are often skilled negotiators who know this dynamic and use it. The sellers are usually people who just want to move a sofa. That information asymmetry consistently favours the buyer.

Safety and Logistics: The Things People Don’t Say Out Loud

Having strangers come to your home is part of the Marketplace model and most transactions proceed without incident. But it’s worth thinking about clearly.

You’re inviting people you don’t know, whose identity you cannot verify, into your home — often while coordinating a time window in advance. For the majority of Edmonton sellers, this is fine. For others, particularly people living alone or in specific circumstances, the calculation is different.

The logistics piece is more universally relevant. When a buyer arrives to pick up a large sectional, they often haven’t fully accounted for how to move it. You may find yourself helping carry a 200-pound sofa down a stairwell, through a hallway, and into a vehicle — without any agreement about liability if someone gets hurt in the process. This is not a hypothetical. Furniture is heavy, stairwells are awkward, and the informal nature of a Marketplace transaction means there are no terms about who is responsible for what.

Payment fraud is a documented and growing problem in Canada. The reverse e-transfer scam — where a buyer sends what looks exactly like an Interac e-transfer notification but is actually a phishing link designed to capture your banking credentials — has been flagged by Ottawa police, the RCMP, and Canadian financial institutions as an increasingly common fraud. Cash in hand on pickup is the only form of payment that eliminates this risk entirely.

The “I’ll send my guy to pick it up” variant — where a buyer pays upfront via a method that later reverses, and sends someone else to collect — is a related pattern. Overpayment scams, where a buyer sends more than the asking price and requests the difference back before the original payment clears, follow a similar logic.

None of this means every Marketplace buyer is a scammer. Most aren’t. But the risk is real enough that cash in hand on pickup is the only form of payment that eliminates it entirely.

The Emotional Friction Is Legitimate

Most people selling quality furniture aren’t professional resellers. They’re people who bought something thoughtfully, owned it well, and are now trying to sell it — often because of a move, a renovation, or a life change. The emotional experience of that is not neutral.

Haggling with strangers over something you paid $5,000 for — something that’s been a central piece of your home — is unpleasant in a way that’s difficult to fully explain until you do it. The lowball offers don’t just feel financially disrespectful. They feel like a mischaracterization of something you value.

This doesn’t mean you shouldn’t sell on Marketplace. It means the experience is not as frictionless as listing a piece of IKEA furniture. For higher-quality pieces, the emotional cost of the process is a real variable.

What the Alternative Looks Like

There is a simpler path: selling to a curated buyer who comes to you, pays immediately in cash, handles all removal, and closes the transaction in a single visit. You don’t hold the piece for anyone. You don’t manage a message thread. You don’t renegotiate on pickup day.

The tradeoff is that a curated buyer is purchasing at wholesale rather than retail — the offer reflects the work and risk they’re absorbing to clean, photograph, market, and resell the piece. You’ll net less than you would in a best-case Marketplace sale.

What you get in exchange is certainty, speed, and none of the above.

Whether that tradeoff makes sense depends on how much your time costs, how urgently you need the piece gone, and how much tolerance you have for the Marketplace experience described in this article. For some sellers — particularly those with a timeline, with a high-value piece, or who simply don’t want to manage the process — it’s the right call.

The Marketplace process is real work. Multiple weeks of messaging, no-shows, price pressure, and emotional friction. If that sounds like more than you’re willing to manage, the direct buyer option closes the transaction in days with certainty and no coordination on your end.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much will I get selling my sofa on Facebook Marketplace Edmonton?

It depends on brand, condition, photos, and how much patience you have. In practice, most sellers end up below their initial asking price — not because the piece isn’t worth more, but because the process wears them down. Lowball offers, slow engagement, and the temptation to just be done with it consistently pull final sale prices below what a well-maintained quality piece should realistically command.

Is it safe to have strangers pick up furniture from my home?

For most Edmonton sellers, most of the time, yes. The majority of Marketplace transactions complete without incident. That said, it’s reasonable to take basic precautions: have another person present for large-item pickups if possible, confirm the buyer’s identity and profile before sharing your exact address, and don’t arrange a pickup with someone whose profile has no history. Payment fraud is a genuine and growing risk in Canada — the reverse e-transfer scam in particular has been flagged by Canadian police forces as increasingly common. Accept cash in hand on pickup to eliminate payment risk entirely.

How long does it take to sell furniture on Facebook Marketplace?

For quality furniture in the mid-to-high price range, expect 2–4 weeks from listing to completed sale, assuming the piece sells at all. High-volume, lower-priced items can move in days. The timeline is affected by multiple variables beyond your control. Budget for the process to take longer than you expect and plan your schedule accordingly if you have a firm deadline.

What are the biggest mistakes people make selling furniture on Marketplace?

Underpricing upfront in hopes of a quick sale (it often just anchors buyers at the wrong number and invites even lower offers), agreeing to hold a piece for a buyer before payment is confirmed (no-shows are common), and accepting e-transfer before the buyer picks up without auto-deposit enabled (fraud risk).
