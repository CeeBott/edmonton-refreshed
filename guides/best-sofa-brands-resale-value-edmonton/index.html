Buyer’s Guide

Which Sofa and Sectional Brands Hold Their Value? What We’ve Seen in Edmonton

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

“Holds its value” can mean three different things, and a sofa or sectional can do well on one and poorly on another. This guide separates them, explains what affects each, and shares what we’ve seen in pieces we’ve bought and sold. These are observations from our own pieces, not a ranking of brands across the Edmonton market.

The Short Answer

If you want names, these are the sofa and sectional brands we’d look at first, from the pieces we’ve handled: B&B Italia, Natuzzi (Italia and Editions), Rove Concepts, Crate & Barrel, EQ3, Pottery Barn, West Elm, American Leather, Brentwood Classics, and Urban Barn, with La-Z-Boy considered selectively. Buyers recognize them, their models can usually be identified and compared with what’s sold new, and most of the pieces we’ve bought from them were well built and had held up. That’s an informed preference, not a measured ranking: the particular model and its condition still decide what any one piece is worth. The rest of this guide explains why.

Three Ways to Measure Resale Value

  • The dollar amount it sells for. A sofa that cost a great deal new can sell for more dollars used than a modest one, even after losing much of its price.
  • The share of its original price it keeps. That depends on what it cost new as much as on what it sells for used, so a very expensive piece doesn’t necessarily keep a larger share.
  • How readily it attracts a suitable buyer. A familiar model in a common size can suit many buyers; a large, expensive, or distinctive piece may suit a smaller group who are looking for it specifically.

Construction quality affects how a sofa holds up, which affects its condition when you sell. But a well-built sofa isn’t automatically easy to sell or worth a high share of what it cost.

What Affects What a Used Sofa or Sectional Sells For

  • Whether it can be identified. A label or receipt naming the maker and model lets buyers look it up and compare it with similar listings.
  • Its condition, including wear, fading, odour, and anything that needs repair.
  • Its size and configuration, and how many homes it suits.
  • Its upholstery and colour, and whether they suit what buyers are looking for.
  • How recognizable the brand is. A well-known name makes a piece easier to identify and compare. Limited recognition makes that harder, but it doesn’t mean the piece is poorly built or has no resale value.
  • How many similar pieces are for sale at the same time.

Brand by Brand: What We’ve Seen

These notes come from sofas and sectionals we’ve bought and sold, and from what each maker publishes about its own furniture. They run roughly from the top of the market down.

B&B Italia

The B&B Italia piece we’ve handled is a Charles sectional. Its label reads Made in Italy, Modello Charles, and Disegno Antonio Citterio, which is exactly what a buyer needs to confirm the model. B&B Italia’s technical information lists the Charles frame as tubular steel and steel profiles rather than wood. At its new price, a Charles suits buyers who know the design and want it specifically, and the label is what lets them trust a used one. Our B&B Italia guide covers the brand in depth.

Natuzzi Italia and Natuzzi Editions

Natuzzi was founded by Pasquale Natuzzi in Apulia in 1959. The company says it runs its own tannery in Italy and makes furniture in its Italian factories and in its own factories in China, Brazil, and Romania. Natuzzi Italia is the higher line and Natuzzi Editions the broader one, so check the label for which line a piece is and where it was made. In our experience these leather pieces are among the easiest to identify and compare. We’ve bought several Editions sectionals, including the Cesare and two Saggezzas, and a matching pair of Natuzzi Italia loveseats about fifteen years old that were still clean and supple, with an even patina. Our Natuzzi guide covers the differences between the two lines.

Rove Concepts

Rove Concepts, which has a showroom in Vancouver, designs and sells the Milo, the only Rove model we’ve bought so far. Rove’s product page lists a kiln-dried hardwood frame with doweled joints and reinforced corner blocks. We’ve bought two Milos: a six-piece in Pearl Chatou bouclé and a four-piece in a grey performance fabric. Its modules sit side by side rather than locking together, so a set can be rearranged, and we photographed ours in more than one layout so buyers could judge the fit. Rove has discontinued some Milo fabrics, so a buyer hoping to add matching pieces later may not find the same one. Our Rove Concepts guide covers the brand.

Crate & Barrel

We’ve had more Crate & Barrel pieces than most brands: a Gather 99-inch Deep sofa, a Rochelle sofa and chair, a matched pair of Lounge bench sofas, and an Aris two-piece sectional. The frame held on every one. Crate & Barrel’s Canadian site lists the Lounge as benchmade with kiln-dried hardwood and made in the USA, and the Aris with a hardwood frame, also made in the USA; the tag on our Gather named its maker, Design Foundry, in Hickory, North Carolina. Labels vary by collection, and one Lounge label printed a delivery date rather than a manufacture date. Condition still decided value: the Rochelle was solid at eight years old but sun-faded, and graded Fair. Our Crate & Barrel guide covers what we learned from each piece.

EQ3

EQ3 makes its custom upholstery in Winnipeg. Its Replay page describes the frame as pinned, glued, and screwed in its Winnipeg factory, and warrants frames and interior construction for life (25 years), cushions for two years, fabric covers for one, and leather covers for five. If you’re buying used, ask whether any coverage passes to a second owner. We’ve bought a 99-inch Replay in a stone-grey fabric, the heaviest stationary sofa we’ve handled, and a Remi in Evoque Stone top-grain leather. Our EQ3 guide covers both models.

Pottery Barn and West Elm

Pottery Barn and West Elm are sister brands under Williams-Sonoma. The Pottery Barn piece we’ve handled is a contract-grade Turner Square Arm sofa in top-grain aniline leather with a wax finish. Pottery Barn lists the Turner as imported, and ours carried a Made in China label on the underside. Its leather and frame were better than we expected, and at 44 inches deep it was larger than its photos suggested. From West Elm, we’ve bought a matched pair of Jodie wing chairs in nubuck leather, delivered new by West Elm in July 2026 and sold together as a pair. Our Pottery Barn guide covers the Turner in detail.

American Leather

The American Leather Carson sectional we bought was built in Dallas, Texas, in July 2015, as three modules that separate for moving. We also bought a Tuscany sofa about fourteen years old that kept very minor surface cracking after conditioning, which shows that a well-regarded name doesn’t protect a piece from how it has been cared for.

Brentwood Classics

Brentwood Classics is a Canadian maker. The Dorado sofa we bought, in Capetown Antico aniline leather, was in excellent condition. The brand isn’t as widely recognized as some on this list, so we listed it by its model and leather names, which gives buyers something specific to look up.

Urban Barn

We consider Urban Barn piece by piece. We’ve bought a Preston sofa with two matching chairs in distressed top-grain leather, and a Karis sofa that had been a new floor model.

CB2 and Article

We consider select pieces from both. Each sells a range of models at different prices and with different construction, so look up the specific model’s specification rather than judging by the brand.

La-Z-Boy

La-Z-Boy makes a wide range, and we consider its leather and premium pieces selectively. We’ve bought an Emric sectional with a right-facing chaise. On reclining models, test every mechanism before you buy or list one.

Mass-market and less familiar brands

Structube, IKEA, Ashley, and many brands buyers won’t recognize also come up in Edmonton’s used market. Each sells a range of models at different prices and with different construction, so a brand name tells you where to start looking, not what a particular piece is. Limited recognition makes a piece harder to identify and compare, but it doesn’t mean it’s poorly built or has no resale value.

What the Pieces Have Taught Us

  • Being able to name the model helps. When we know the model, size, and upholstery, we can find what the same piece costs new today, and so can a buyer.
  • Configuration matters for sectionals. Showing a modular piece in more than one layout lets buyers judge whether it suits their room.
  • Condition decides more than the name or the age. A twelve-year-old Natuzzi Editions Cesare that had sat nearly unused in a basement graded Good after cleaning, with a few very small marks remaining, while the Tuscany and the Rochelle graded Fair for cracking and fading.
  • A luxury name suits a specialist buyer. That says something about a piece’s buyer pool, not that luxury pieces sell more easily.

Construction Belongs to the Model, Not the Brand

Frames, suspension, cushions, and upholstery vary from model to model, even within one brand. To judge a piece, find the model’s published specification and check the piece’s present condition.

  • Frames: solid hardwood, plywood, and steel can all be built well or badly. “Kiln-dried” means the wood was dried to a controlled moisture content, which helps reduce later shrinkage and movement; on its own it doesn’t say what the frame is made of or how it was joined. Our guide to judging a sofa’s quality covers the checks.
  • Suspension: sinuous springs, coil springs, and webbing systems differ in design and quality from one maker and model to the next. Check the specification for the model rather than assuming one from the brand.
  • Leather: the hide layer (full-grain, top-grain, split), the surface treatment, and the finish (aniline, semi-aniline, pigmented) are separate attributes. Bonded leather is a composite, not a grade. Our guide to leather sofas explains each.
  • Fabric: polyester and blends vary widely in weave and durability, and “performance fabric” is the maker’s claim that a fabric resists stains or cleans easily. Our guide to fabric sofas covers what the labels mean.

Working Out the Cost of Owning a Sofa or Sectional

Resale value is one part of what a sofa costs you over the years you own it:

(Purchase price + delivery + any repairs − what you sell it for) ÷ the years you own it

A hypothetical example. A $3,500 sofa kept for ten years and sold for $500 costs ($3,500 − $500) ÷ 10 = $300 a year. A $1,200 sofa kept for four years and then given away costs $1,200 ÷ 4 = $300 a year. With these assumptions they cost the same per year; change how long you keep each one or what it sells for, and the result changes. The figures are for illustration only.

Practical Advice

If you’re buying, choose a sofa that fits your room and how you live, and inspect the particular piece. Treat possible resale value as a secondary consideration. Our guide to buying a used sofa covers what to check.

If you’re selling, identify the model from the label or receipt, document its condition honestly with clear photos, compare it with listings for the same or similar models, and choose a price for your timeline. Our guide to pricing a used sofa walks through it. If it’s a premium sofa or sectional, you can also ask us for an offer; it reflects our assessment of the piece and our own costs, so it’s not necessarily what you could achieve in a private sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which sofa brands have the best resale value?

It depends what you mean by resale value: the dollar amount a sofa sells for, the share of its original price it keeps, or how readily it finds a suitable buyer. These can point to different pieces. In the pieces we’ve handled, the model, its condition, and whether it can be identified have mattered more than the brand alone. Recognized names such as Natuzzi, Crate & Barrel, EQ3, and Rove Concepts are easier for buyers to identify and compare.

How do EQ3 and CB2 compare?

Both sell a range of models at different prices and with different construction, so neither can be judged as a whole. Compare the specific models you’re considering: the maker’s published specifications, the piece’s condition, and listings for the same or similar models.

What does kiln-dried hardwood mean?

“Kiln-dried” on its own describes how wood was dried: in a kiln, to a controlled moisture content, which helps reduce later shrinkage and movement. It doesn’t tell you what the frame is made of, since engineered panels can be made from kiln-dried wood too. “Kiln-dried hardwood” is more specific, and a full specification tells you where hardwood and other materials are used in the frame. Check the model’s specification and the piece’s condition too.

Is a used premium-brand sofa better than a new budget one?

It can be, if the particular piece fits your space, is comfortable, and is in good condition at a total cost you’re happy with. A premium name doesn’t guarantee that, and a less expensive sofa in good condition may suit you well. Compare the specific pieces.

Do leather sofas hold their value better than fabric?

Not as a rule. It depends on the specific piece: the type of leather or fabric, its condition, and whether buyers can identify it. Bonded leather is a composite that tends to peel, which counts against condition, and fading, staining, or cracking count against any upholstery.
