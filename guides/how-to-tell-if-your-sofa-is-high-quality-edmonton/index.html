How to Tell if a Sofa or Sectional Is High Quality: Frame, Springs, Cushions, and Upholstery

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

A high-quality sofa or sectional comes down to five things: the frame, the springs or suspension under the seat, the cushions, the upholstery, and how well it was put together. No single material makes a piece high quality. Solid hardwood, plywood, and steel can all be built well or badly, and the same goes for foam, springs, and fabric. To judge a piece, find out what model it is and what its maker publishes about it, then check its present condition.

The frame matters most because it’s the hardest part to fix. Springs, cushions, and covers can often be repaired or replaced, though how practical that is depends on how the piece is built and how much work it needs. The checks below are the same whether you’re buying a used sofa or selling your own Natuzzi sectional or Crate & Barrel sofa. If you’re selling, knowing how your piece is built lets you answer a buyer’s questions and describe it accurately.

What Makes a Sofa or Sectional Frame High Quality

Many good sofas combine materials. Quality depends on:

  • Material grade. Thick, furniture-grade plywood and thin, low-grade sheet are both sold as “plywood”.
  • Design. How the seat’s load reaches the legs, and how the arms and back are tied in.
  • Joinery and reinforcement. Dowels, glued and screwed joints, and corner blocks hold better than staples alone.
  • Workmanship and care. How well this particular piece was built, and how it has been treated since.

About “kiln-dried”: It describes wood dried in a kiln to a controlled moisture content, helping reduce later shrinkage and movement. Engineered panels can also be made from kiln-dried wood, so the term does not necessarily mean a solid-wood frame. On its own, it tells you little about how well the frame was designed or assembled.

Solid hardwood

Strong and holds screws well. Its weak point is the joints: a hardwood frame held together mostly with staples can loosen.

Plywood and engineered hardwood

Plywood is layers of wood glued with the grain crossing, which resists splitting and warping. Its grade, thickness, and how the frame is assembled decide how well it holds up; the weak point is thin, low-grade sheet, which can split at screws or come apart at the edges.

“Engineered hardwood” and “engineered wood” are broader labels. Engineered wood covers several manufactured products, plywood among them, so the label alone doesn’t tell you which one a frame uses. The maker’s specification, or the maker itself, is the way to find out. Good sofas use engineered wood, including two we’ve bought (below).

Steel

Used in some contemporary sofas, often moulded into foam, because it allows slim arms and backs that would be weak in wood.

Particleboard and MDF

Wood particles or fibres pressed with resin, common in low-cost furniture. They hold screws poorly, weaken when wet, and a loose joint is hard to repair, so they’re a poor choice for the parts of a frame that carry weight.

Springs and Suspension

The suspension sits on the frame under the seat cushions and carries most of your weight. You usually can’t see it, because deck fabric or a dust cover hides it, but the maker’s specification often names it. The common types:

  • Sinuous springs, also called no-sag or zigzag springs: S-shaped steel wires running from front to back, clipped to the frame. They’re widely used, including on several well-built sofas we’ve bought. The wire’s thickness and spacing matter; a lower gauge number means a thicker wire.
  • Eight-way hand-tied coils: coil springs tied to each other by hand in eight directions. It’s a traditional, labour-intensive method, mostly found on higher-priced pieces.
  • Webbing: elastic or woven strips stretched across the seat, used in some designs, especially slimmer contemporary ones. How good the webbing is and how tightly it’s fitted both matter.

Any of these can be done well or badly, so check the condition rather than ranking the type. Sit in every seat and feel for one that sinks lower or feels softer than its neighbours. With a cushion off, press the deck and feel for a broken or loose spring, a spring that has come off its clip, or webbing that has stretched and sags. On a Crate & Barrel Rochelle we bought, the sinuous springs were visible through its thin dust cover; on most pieces you’ll be going by feel and the specification.

Cushions

Cushions are what you feel first, and they wear sooner than the frame or springs. Makers build them in a few ways:

  • Foam, often in layers of different densities. Higher-density foam generally keeps its shape longer than low-density foam of a similar feel.
  • Foam wrapped in fibre or a down blend, which softens the surface over a supportive core.
  • Innerspring cores: small coil springs inside the cushion, wrapped in foam and fibre.
  • Feather and down blends, which are soft and need regular plumping.
  • Fibre fill, common in back cushions. It’s light, so a fibre-filled back cushion can feel almost hollow when you pick it up; that’s how it’s made, not a sign of a cheap piece.

Makers often publish their cushion construction. Rove Concepts lists the Milo’s cushions as three layers of high-density foam topped with goose feathers, and EQ3 describes the Replay’s as layered foam of varied density wrapped in polyester fibre. The law label sewn under a cushion or the deck lists the filling materials too: the label on a Crate & Barrel Aris we bought listed its back cushion fill as 100% polyester fibre.

To check cushions, press each seat cushion and let go: foam should recover promptly and evenly, while a down blend needs plumping back up. Cushions that sag in the middle or stay flat are worn. If the covers unzip, look at the inserts. A worn insert in a removable cushion can often be replaced. A fixed or attached seat is a job for an upholsterer, and reupholstering or repairing the suspension is possible but can be expensive relative to what the piece is worth. If a repair would decide whether you buy, get a quote first.

Upholstery and Workmanship

The cover tells you a lot about how carefully a piece was made. Look for:

  • Seams and stitching that run straight, with even, tight stitches and no loose threads at the arm fronts and cushion corners.
  • Patterns and stripes that line up across cushions and from the seat to the back.
  • Welting or piping that sits straight and is intact.
  • Cushion covers that fit snugly, without slack or bunching, and zips that work.
  • The back and sides, which on a well-made piece are finished as neatly as the front, with a fitted dust cover underneath.
  • Legs that are firmly attached and sit level.

The upholstery material matters too. For leather, the hide layer, surface treatment, and finish are separate things; our guide to leather sofas and sectionals explains each. For fabric, some makers publish an abrasion rating in double rubs or Martindale cycles. It’s a lab test of how well a fabric resists rubbing, useful for comparing fabrics but not a prediction of lifespan. Our guide to fabric sofas covers fabric types and cleaning codes.

What the Makers Publish for Four Pieces We’ve Bought

Construction varies by model, even within one brand, and some retailers sell many manufacturers’ furniture. So look up the model, not the brand. Here is what the makers publish for four pieces we’ve bought:

  • B&B Italia Charles. B&B Italia’s technical information lists the internal frame as tubular steel and steel profiles, padded with cold-shaped polyurethane foam. See the Charles sectional.
  • Crate & Barrel Gather. Crate & Barrel’s product pages describe the frame as benchmade with FSC-certified engineered hardwood that is kiln-dried to prevent warping. For the Gather Deep, its Canadian page also lists sinuous wire spring suspension, innerspring seat cushions, and fibre-filled back cushions. See this Gather.
  • Pottery Barn Turner. Pottery Barn’s UK product page describes an engineered wood frame with corner-block construction, no-sag sinuous springs, and down-wrapped cushions, which matches what our listing recorded. Specifications can differ between regions and years, so check the page for the piece you’re looking at. See the Turner we sold.
  • Rove Concepts Milo. Rove Concepts’ spec sheets list a kiln-dried hardwood frame with doweled joints and reinforced corner blocks, and 8-gauge sinuous springs. See the Milo we sold.

How to Check the Quality of a Sofa or Sectional Before You Buy: A Quick First Inspection

A quick look can identify the model and reveal obvious problems with the frame, springs, cushions, and upholstery. It won’t tell you what the frame is made of: looking at the deck, tapping a rail, or listening for creaks won’t identify the material.

1. Find the label and photograph it. Check under the seat cushions, on the deck, and underneath. The maker’s label often gives the brand and model, and sometimes a date. With the model name you can look up the maker’s specification. The white law label identifies the filling materials and may include other details, but it usually doesn’t tell you how the frame is built. If you’re buying, ask for the original receipt too.

2. Take a seat cushion off and look at the deck. On most sofas you’ll see deck fabric, not the frame. Check for tears, a seat that sits lower than its neighbours, and broken or loose springs you can feel through the fabric.

3. Watch for movement in the frame. If the owner agrees and the piece can be handled safely, with help if it’s heavy, lift one front corner a few inches. A joint that opens, a leg that shifts, or unusual flex or twist needs a closer look. It’s one observation alongside the other checks; it can’t confirm the condition of a frame you can’t see.

4. Sit on every seat and press the arms and back. A creak or click means something is moving: a loose joint, a cracked rail, a spring clip, or on a recliner the mechanism. Find out which before you buy.

5. Press each seat cushion and read the law label. Foam should recover promptly and evenly; a down blend needs plumping. The label lists the filling materials.

6. Look at the seams and the finish, including the back and sides: straight seams, even stitching, patterns that line up, and covers that fit.

7. Look at the legs and the underside you can see. Check that the legs are tight and the piece sits level, and note any repairs, splits, or water marks.

8. Ask the owner the model, when and where it was bought, how many moves it has been through, and whether anything has been repaired.

Weight, Price, and Brand Are Hints, Not Proof

A heavy sofa often has more solid wood, springs, or dense foam in it. The heaviest stationary sofa we’ve handled, an EQ3 Replay, is built on a kiln-dried hardwood frame. But weight also follows size and what’s inside: a long sofa outweighs a short one, and a power recliner is heavy because of its mechanisms. Price tells you what was paid, not how the model was built, and construction varies from model to model within one brand. Treat all three as reasons to look closer, then go by the model’s specification and the piece’s condition.

What to Do With What You Find

A sound engineered frame

The label names the model, the maker lists engineered hardwood, and the piece passes every check. Treat it as a sound frame and decide on the cushions, upholstery, size, and price.

A damaged hardwood frame

The maker lists solid hardwood, but an arm joint opens when you lift the corner, or a cracked rail has dropped one seat. The material doesn’t make up for the damage. Get a repair quote from an upholsterer or walk away. Our guide to furniture condition grades covers how damage affects a piece’s grade.

Construction you can’t verify

No label, no paperwork, and the owner doesn’t remember the maker. Judge it on condition alone and pay less for the uncertainty. Our guide to buying a used sofa in Edmonton covers the rest of the inspection.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a sofa is good quality before buying?

Find the label and look up the model’s published specification for the frame, suspension, and cushions. Then check its condition: sit on every seat, watch for movement at the joints and legs, press the cushions, and look at the seams and finish. Price and brand are signals, but the model and its condition tell you more.

How do I know if a sofa frame is built to last?

Look for what the maker publishes about the model’s frame: the material and its grade, and joinery such as dowels and corner blocks. Then check the frame you’re looking at: it should sit level and stay quiet under weight, with no joint opening or leg shifting when it’s moved. The material name alone, hardwood or plywood, doesn’t settle it.

Can you replace a sofa frame?

Parts of one, usually. An upholsterer can often reglue a loose joint, replace a corner block, or replace a cracked rail. Replacing the whole frame means stripping and re-upholstering the sofa, which is rarely worth it. If you’re buying, treat frame damage as a repair you’ll have to arrange, or walk away.

Is a solid hardwood frame better than plywood?

Not by material alone. Solid hardwood, furniture-grade plywood, and steel can all make durable frames; grade, design, joinery, and workmanship decide it. Crate & Barrel lists the Gather’s frame as engineered hardwood, for example. Thin, low-grade plywood is the weak case.

What does kiln-dried mean on a sofa frame?

Wood dried in a kiln to a controlled moisture content, which reduces later shrinkage and movement. Engineered panels can also be made from kiln-dried wood, so it doesn’t necessarily mean solid wood, and on its own it tells you little about how well the frame was designed or assembled.

Can you tell frame quality from a sofa’s price or brand?

Not reliably. Construction varies from model to model within one brand, and some retailers sell many manufacturers’ furniture. The model’s published specification, the label, and the frame’s condition tell you more.

How can I find out what my sofa’s frame is made of?

Find the label, usually under a seat cushion, on the deck, or underneath, and photograph it. Use the maker and model name to look up the published specification or ask the manufacturer. An original receipt helps too. Looking at the deck or listening for creaks won’t tell you the material.

Are sinuous springs or eight-way hand-tied coils better?

Both can be durable. Eight-way hand-tied coils are a traditional, labour-intensive method found mostly on higher-priced pieces. Sinuous springs are widely used and support well when the wire and spacing suit the design; the Crate & Barrel Gather Deep, Pottery Barn Turner, and Rove Concepts Milo all list them. The condition of the springs in the piece you’re looking at matters more than the type.

What should good sofa cushions be made of?

There’s no single right answer. Good cushions are often layered foam, foam wrapped in fibre or a down blend, or an innerspring core, and back cushions are often fibre-filled. The law label lists the filling, and the maker’s specification often describes the construction. In a used piece, check that foam recovers promptly and evenly. A worn insert in a removable cushion can often be replaced; a fixed seat needs an upholsterer.

Is a heavier sofa better quality?

Not necessarily. Weight often comes from solid wood, springs, or dense foam, but it also follows size and mechanisms: a long sofa outweighs a short one, and a power recliner is heavy because of its motors. Treat weight as a reason to look closer, then check the model’s specification and the piece’s condition.

Why do some sofas use plywood or particleboard instead of solid wood?

Cost is one reason: particleboard and thin plywood are cheaper. Design is another: furniture-grade plywood is stable and strong in sheet form, and well-built sofas use engineered wood, such as the engineered hardwood Crate & Barrel lists for the Gather. The grade and how the frame is joined matter more than the material’s name.

Does a good frame mean the sofa will last?

It means the hardest part to fix is sound. Springs, cushions, and upholstery wear separately. A worn insert in a removable cushion can often be replaced, but reupholstering, rebuilding a fixed seat, or repairing the suspension can be expensive relative to the piece’s value, so get a quote before counting on it.
