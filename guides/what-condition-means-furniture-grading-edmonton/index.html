How to Tell What Condition Your Sofa or Sectional Is In (And What It Means for Selling)

Edmonton Refreshed · Collin Bottrell

Condition is one of the biggest factors in what a used sofa or sectional is worth, and in whether a buyer trusts your listing enough to come and see it. This guide sets out the four condition grades we use when we buy and list pieces, how to test your own sofa or sectional against them, and how to tell normal wear from damage.

The grades cover visible wear, cushion condition, and odour: how much use a piece shows. Whether it’s structurally sound is assessed separately, covered below, and so is how well it was built.

Quick Condition Check (60 Seconds)

  • Sit down. Do the cushions hold their intended shape and support, and does every comparable seat feel about the same? Feather or down cushions that need plumping are normal; flattening that plumping or turning doesn’t fix is wear.
  • Look at the surface. Do you see pilling, fading, creasing, cracking, or stains?
  • Smell it up close. Neutral, or a noticeable odour? That includes pleasant scents like perfume, laundry detergent, or air freshener, which a buyer may not find pleasant.
  • First impression. From across the room, does it look clean and little-used, or clearly lived on?

If you hesitated on any of these, read the grade descriptions below before you choose one, and note what made you hesitate. It belongs in your listing.

Why the Grade Matters When You Sell

Every buyer asks the same things: have the cushions held their shape, is there visible wear or staining, is there an odour, does it feel solid? “Good condition” on its own doesn’t answer them, because it means different things to different people. To an owner it can mean the sofa still works; to a buyer it can mean it looks nearly new.

A grade on a shared scale, with the specific wear described and photographed, lets a buyer judge the piece before they travel to see it. When you’ve tested the cushions, looked at the high-wear areas, and checked the smell, you can answer their questions directly instead of finding things out alongside them. A buyer who arrives expecting one grade and finds another may offer less than the price you agreed, or leave without buying.

A piece doesn’t need every sign in a row to sit at that grade, and the specific wear on any one piece belongs in its own description.

How to Grade Your Sofa or Sectional

Grading isn’t about how you feel about the sofa or how carefully you’ve looked after it. It’s about what’s actually there when you sit on it, look at it, and inspect it systematically.

Excellent / Like-New

Cushions: Little or no sign of deterioration. They keep their intended shape and support, whether they’re designed to feel firm or soft, and comparable seats feel the same. Feather or down cushions may need plumping; that’s normal for them, not wear.

Fabric or leather: No visible wear, even in high-friction areas like the cushion sides where hands rest. No pilling, fading, or discolouration. Seams are tight, stitching is intact, and piping is clean.

Crevices and underside: Clean between the cushions, with little sign of use underneath and no scuffs on the legs or base.

Smell: Clean or neutral. If there’s any smell, it’s the fabric or leather itself.

What it means: Next to no signs of use. Honest Excellent condition is uncommon. In our own grading, we reserve “like-new” for pieces that are not only in exceptional condition but also close to new in age.

Very Good

Cushions: Slight settling where people sit most, but they keep their shape and support, and normal plumping or turning restores them.

Fabric or leather: Minimal visible wear: perhaps slight pilling in one area, but not widespread. Leather may have an even patina, a gradual change in colour and sheen from use. Fabric may show minor fading on the back or sides while the seats look good. Seams and stitching are intact.

Crevices and underside: Clean or mostly clean. The underside shows use but no staining or water marks.

Smell: Clean or neutral.

What it means: Minimal signs of use. A buyer sees a well-kept piece with light wear.

Good

Cushions: A seating pattern you can see before you sit, or some difference between the most-used seats and the others. Plumping or turning restores them only partly. Still comfortable and supportive.

Fabric or leather: Wear is visible. Fabric may show pilling on the cushion sides and back, or fading. Leather may show creasing where it flexes and some colour variation from use. There may be a small mark that cleaning didn’t remove.

Crevices and underside: Signs of age underneath, but no water damage or major discolouration.

Smell: Neutral once the room has aired.

What it means: The piece has clearly been used, and it’s structurally sound. Age and use are separate: a twelve-year-old Natuzzi Editions Cesare we bought had sat nearly unused in a basement and graded Good after cleaning, with a few very small marks remaining.

Fair / Heavily Used

Cushions: Substantial loss of shape or support, with flattening or indentations that stay after normal plumping or turning, beyond what’s normal for the cushion design.

Fabric or leather: Heavy wear: pronounced pilling or fading, marks that cleaning didn’t remove, or on leather, deep creasing and surface cracking.

Smell: A smell may remain after cleaning and airing. If it does, describe it.

What it means: Well loved, and still structurally sound. That’s the line: confirmed frame or suspension damage, such as a broken rail or failed springs, can’t be communicated by calling a piece Fair. It has to be disclosed explicitly (see below). A Fair piece can still be a sensible buy at a price that reflects its condition. We’ve bought Fair pieces ourselves, including an eight-year-old Crate & Barrel Rochelle graded Fair for sun-fading.

The Assessment, Step by Step

Sit on the piece and look at it systematically, not casually.

1. The cushion test. Sit as if you’re settling in to watch a show, on every seat. Notice whether the cushions keep their shape and support, whether an indentation stays after you stand and after plumping or turning, and whether the arms and sides feel solid. A seat that’s noticeably softer or lower than comparable seats is worth noting; step 6 covers how to tell where that comes from.

2. The visual sweep. Step back about eight feet, roughly where a buyer stands when they first see it. Does it look clean and kept, or worn? Look at the whole piece, not just the parts you know are fine. If your eye is drawn to a spot, a buyer’s will be too.

3. The high-wear areas. Look at the cushion sides where hands rest, the back where shoulders and heads lean, the seams, the piping, and between the cushions. Pilling, fraying, and small tears show up here first.

4. The material check. On leather, run your hand across it: is it supple or stiff, and are there creases, peeling, or cracks? Patina is normal; cracking and peeling count against condition. On fabric, look at high-friction areas for pilling, discolouration, and thin spots. Our guides to leather sofas and sectionals and fabric sofas cover what to look for in each.

5. The smell test. Leave the room for a while, then come back in and smell the cushions and back up close. Ask what the smell is, not whether there is one. A neutral smell fits any grade. A smell that stays after cleaning and airing, including smoke or pet odour, should be described in your listing; it can affect value more than the grade alone suggests.

6. The structural check. Sit in the middle. Does it feel stable, and does one side sit higher or lower? On a sectional, check each section for a slope or sag. These are observations to investigate, not a diagnosis:

  • A cushion that stays compressed usually points to worn filling.
  • A seat that stays low or uneven with a removable cushion lifted off points beneath the cushion: the suspension, the frame, or how they’re attached. Look and feel through the deck. If the cushions are fixed, don’t take anything apart; note it and have it looked at.
  • Wobbling usually starts with the legs: check that each is tight and its attachment is sound. It doesn’t on its own mean the frame is broken.
  • A joint that visibly moves when you press the arms or back needs a closer look at the frame.

Confirmed frame or suspension damage is a structural problem to disclose, not a lower grade. Our guide to judging a sofa’s quality covers the frame checks in more detail.

Wear vs. Damage: The Critical Distinction

Wear is normal. It’s what happens when a sofa is sat on: minor pilling, fading or colour variation from sun, creasing in leather where it flexes, dust in the crevices, a seating pattern in the cushions, feather or down cushions that need plumping, slightly loose stitching, patina on leather, and light scratches on wooden legs.

Damage goes beyond normal use. Stains that won’t come out, tears in visible or high-traffic areas, peeling leather or cracks that have split the surface, separating seams, broken zippers on removable covers, water stains, pet damage such as claw marks or accident stains, an odour that stays after airing, and broken springs.

Some signs need investigating before you call them either. Cushion filling that stays flat is wear, sometimes heavy wear, and a removable insert can often be replaced. A seat that sits low even with the cushion off, a wobble, or a joint that moves points to the suspension, the frame, the legs, or their attachments; find out which before you describe it.

Why it matters: a buyer looking at a worn piece knows they’re buying used furniture and has adjusted their expectations. A buyer who finds damage they weren’t told about feels misled.

The grade describes how much wear a piece shows. Damage is stated separately: describe it plainly in your listing, with a photo, whatever the grade. Significant damage, such as a broken spring or a frame problem, matters more to a buyer than the grade does.

It’s easy to grade a piece you live with more generously than a buyer will, because you’ve watched it change gradually. Grade it the way someone seeing it for the first time would, and ask someone who doesn’t sit on it every day to look too. Then describe the specific wear in your listing, which tells a buyer more than the grade alone.

A Grade Describes Wear, Not How the Piece Was Built

Condition grading is independent of brand. Two sofas with the same grade can differ a great deal in how they were built, and so in how they’ll hold up from here. The grade tells you how much use shows; the model’s published specification and the structural checks tell you how it’s built. Construction varies from model to model, even within one brand, so look up the model rather than judging by the name. Our guide to sofa and sectional brands that hold their value covers what we’ve seen from specific makers.

That’s why the grade and the build belong together in a listing. “Good condition” on a model whose construction you can name and document gives a buyer more to go on than “good condition” on a piece nobody can identify.

Condition Details Matter More When Buyers Travel

If you’re outside central Edmonton, in St. Albert, Sherwood Park, Spruce Grove, Leduc, or Fort Saskatchewan, a buyer may be driving some distance with a vehicle and help arranged. A clear grade, a plain description of the wear, and close-up photos let them decide before they make the trip, which saves a wasted visit on both sides.

Using the Grade

Once you’ve graded your piece, put the grade and the specific wear in your listing, with photos of both. Use it when you compare your piece with others for sale: price it against similar pieces in similar condition, not the cleanest ones listed. Frequently Asked Questions

How firm should a sofa be?

Firmness is a matter of design and preference: a down-blend seat is meant to feel softer than a firm foam one, and a soft sofa can be in Excellent condition. For judging condition, what matters is that the cushions keep their intended shape and support, that comparable seats feel about the same, and that normal plumping or turning restores them. Feather or down cushions need plumping by design. Flattening that plumping doesn’t fix, or one seat much lower than its neighbours, points to wear.

What does “like new” mean for a used sofa?

Next to no signs of use: cushions that keep their intended shape and support, no visible wear, and a clean or neutral smell. In our own grading, we reserve “like-new” for pieces that are not only in exceptional condition but also close to new in age. It sits within our top grade, Excellent.

What’s the difference between a stain I can live with and one that affects the grade?

A stain affects the grade if a buyer would notice it straight away. If your eye is drawn to it when you sit down, or you’d feel the need to explain it to a friend, include it in your assessment and your listing. A small, faint mark that isn’t visually prominent is ordinary wear.

If my sofa has a tear in a cushion cover but the cushion is fine, how does that affect the grade?

A tear is damage, so describe and photograph it whatever the grade. On a removable cover, it may be fixable by repairing or replacing the cover, which is worth checking with the maker or an upholsterer. On a fixed cover, repair is upholstery work, and it matters more to a buyer.

Are sagging cushions a condition issue or a structural issue?

It depends on the cause. Soft cushions that keep their shape are normal, and feather or down cushions need plumping by design. Cushions that stay flat after plumping or turning point to worn fill, which affects condition and can often be addressed with new inserts in a removable cushion. A seat that stays low or uneven with a removable cushion lifted off points beneath it, to the suspension, frame, or attachments; investigate before you price or buy, and disclose any confirmed damage.

How much does pet or smoke odour affect a sofa’s condition?

A lot, even on a piece that looks perfect. Odour can get into the cushion fill and foam, so cleaning the cover may not remove it. Describe any smell that stays after cleaning and airing, and expect it to weigh on what buyers will pay. If pets lived with the piece, check closely for claw marks and stains as well.

I bought my sofa ten years ago but barely used it. Is it Excellent because it’s low-use?

Not necessarily. Age and use are separate, and the grade describes the wear you can see. Light use helps, but fabric and leather can still change with time and sunlight. A twelve-year-old Natuzzi Editions Cesare we bought had sat nearly unused in a basement and graded Good after cleaning. Grade what’s actually there.

I cleaned my sofa before assessing it. Should I grade it higher?

Grade it as it is after cleaning. Cleaning can remove dirt, some marks, and some odours, which can genuinely change how it looks, but it doesn’t restore flattened cushion filling, fading, cracking, or anything structural.
