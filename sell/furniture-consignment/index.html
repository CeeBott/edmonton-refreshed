    Questions to ask before signing a consignment contract

    If you're talking to a consignment shop, a few questions are worth asking up front so the math is clear before the piece leaves your home.

    • What's the commission rate, and does it apply to the original asking price or the final sale price? Most shops calculate commission against the final-sale-after-markdowns figure, which can be meaningfully lower than the price on the tag at intake.
    • What's the markdown schedule? Many shops apply automatic reductions at 30, 60, and 90 days. Knowing the schedule helps you estimate the likely payout range before signing.
    • Are there intake, photography, or storage fees? Some shops charge a flat fee at intake regardless of whether the piece sells, and a few charge for professional photography or extended storage if the piece sits past the listing window.
    • Who delivers the piece — and what happens if it doesn't sell? Most shops require the seller to deliver. If the piece doesn't sell within the term, you'll usually need to retrieve it (sometimes with a fee) or accept donation or disposal.
    • What's the payout schedule after a sale? Most shops pay 30 to 60 days after a sale clears. Some hold longer.

    If the answers add up favourably for your piece, consignment can be a strong path. If they don't, a direct offer is one form to fill out away.

    Why Edmonton sellers contact us instead of consigning

    The piece doesn’t fit a shop’s floor

    Sectionals and large sofas often exceed the display space a consignment shop can dedicate. A direct buyer doesn’t carry that constraint.

    The commission math doesn’t work

    A 40–50% split on a marked-down final price typically nets less than sellers initially picture. A direct offer removes the effort of transport and the uncertain timeline, often for similar payout.

    No appetite for the 60–90 day wait

    Some sellers want a firm number on day one rather than a maybe-payout three months out. Direct buyout closes the same week.

    Already tried consignment without a sale

    When a piece has been on the floor and returned unsold — or sold for a fraction of the initial price — a buyout often makes more sense than re-listing.

    How furniture consignment typically works in Edmonton

    The local consignment model is fairly consistent across shops. You bring (or sometimes deliver) your piece. The shop accepts or declines it based on what they think they can move. If accepted, the piece sits on their floor for a fixed window — often 60 to 90 days — and you receive a percentage of whatever it eventually sells for. Commissions usually fall between 40 and 50 percent of the final sale price.

    Most shops apply automatic price reductions over time. A piece that hasn't moved at 30 days drops 10 to 15 percent. Another reduction at 60 days. By the end of the window, the piece either sells at a meaningfully lower number than initially listed, or returns to you (often at your cost to retrieve). Some shops also charge a non-refundable intake or photography fee.

    None of this is unfair on the shop's part — floor space costs money, staff time costs money, and they carry the risk of a piece not moving at all. It's just the structure, and it's worth understanding before you choose between channels.

    Where consignment makes sense

    Consignment can produce strong outcomes for the right piece. The conditions where it tends to work:

    • The piece is genuinely high-end — an Italian or European-built sofa, a recognized designer name, or a heritage Canadian make in excellent condition
    • The colour and fabric are neutral enough to appeal to a wide buyer pool
    • You have somewhere to keep the piece or are not under time pressure (no move date, no replacement arriving, no estate to clear)
    • You're comfortable with an uncertain payout date and a final number that may shift downward through the listing window
    • You can transport it to the shop — or pay for delivery to them

    If those conditions hold, a strong piece on consignment can outperform a direct buyout. The shop has the storefront, the foot traffic, and the time. We won't pretend otherwise.

    Where a direct buyout fits better

    The trade with consignment is time and certainty. Sellers who come to us are often in situations where neither is available:

    • A move date is locked in. Possession changes in 30 days; the sectional has to be gone, not pending.
    • An estate needs to be settled. Multiple pieces, family coordination, and a finite timeline don't pair well with a 90-day waiting period.
    • A new piece is on its way. Once the new sofa arrives, the old one becomes a logistics problem in the living room.
    • Transport isn't realistic. Sectionals, large sofas, and modular pieces are difficult to load into a personal vehicle, and most consignment shops won't pick up.
    • The seller wants a single, paid-out conversation. Photos in, offer back, pickup scheduled, paid at the door. No floor displays, no price reductions over time, no return-of-unsold-piece logistics.

    Our offers are often surprisingly close to what the same piece would eventually net on consignment after commissions, markdowns, and months on the floor. The difference is that we buy directly: no waiting, no uncertainty, and no risk of the piece not selling. For many sellers, that certainty is worth more than chasing the absolute highest price.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between consignment and an outright buyout?

    Consignment means a shop displays your piece and you get paid only when (and if) it sells, typically minus a 40 to 50 percent commission. An outright buyout means we pay you at pickup based on what we expect to be able to resell the piece for. Consignment can produce a higher final number on the right piece; a buyout produces certainty and immediate payment.

    How long does furniture typically take to sell on consignment in Edmonton?

    Most local consignment shops give pieces 60 to 90 days on the floor before applying automatic price reductions or returning the item. Faster-moving categories (smaller accent pieces, neutral upholstery, recognizable brands) tend to clear inside that window. Larger sectionals or pieces with strong colour or fabric specificity can take longer or not move at all.

    Do you handle pickup, or do I need to deliver the piece?

    We handle pickup fully — truck, blankets, straps, and the lifting. You don't move the piece. Most consignment shops in Edmonton require sellers to deliver, which is part of why a direct buyout suits people who don't want to coordinate transport themselves.

    How quickly do you respond after I send photos?

    Most offers go out the same day we receive the photos. From initial submission to pickup is typically 72 hours or less when needed, scheduled around your availability.

    What kinds of pieces are not a fit for either consignment or your buyout?

    Mass-market pieces (Ashley, IKEA, Leon's, The Brick), bonded or faux leather, items with significant pet damage or staining, and broken mechanical pieces (hide-a-beds, power recliners with motor issues) are difficult through both channels. Donation or removal services are usually the more honest path for those.

    Where can I sell my furniture in Edmonton without using consignment?

    Edmonton Refreshed buys quality sofas and sectionals directly from sellers across Edmonton, St. Albert, Sherwood Park, Spruce Grove, Leduc, and Fort Saskatchewan — no consignment timeline, no markdown schedule. Send photos through the form on this page; we reply with a firm offer today and cover pickup at no cost. Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji are the other direct-sale channels, with the trade-off of managing the listing process yourself.

    How much commission do consignment shops in Edmonton charge?

    Most Edmonton consignment shops charge 40 to 50 percent of the final sale price, sometimes higher on lower-priced items. That commission comes out of whatever the piece sells for after any markdowns the shop applies during the listing window — not the original asking price. A direct buyout has no commission, and the price you accept is the price you receive.

    Do I have to commit to anything when I send photos?

    No. Submitting photos starts a conversation. You're not obligated to accept any offer we make, and there is no follow-up pressure if it's not the right fit. Many sellers use a direct offer as a floor reference before deciding which channel to use.
